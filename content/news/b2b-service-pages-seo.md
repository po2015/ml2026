A manufacturer launches their new website with a single "Our Services" page: four short paragraphs, one per capability, a stock photo, and a contact link at the bottom. Six months later, the [Search Console](/news/google-search-console-exporters/) data tells the story: the page ranks for the company name and nothing else. Meanwhile a competitor — objectively a smaller operation — ranks page one for "custom CNC machining aluminum enclosures" with a dedicated page that walks through materials, tolerances, equipment, and lead times. The buyer who searched that phrase had a drawing ready to send. She sent it to the competitor. This is the most common structural failure on manufacturer websites: capabilities bundled into one page that ranks for nothing and converts nobody. Here's the structure that fixes it.

## One page, one job, one query cluster

The core principle: **a page can only rank for one intent.** Google matches pages to queries, and a page about four services is a mediocre answer to all four queries. "Custom CNC machining," "OEM cable assembly," and "powder coating services" are three different searches by three different buyers — they need three different pages.

This isn't SEO pedantry; it's how buyers search. An engineer with a sourcing project searches the *capability*, often with qualifiers — material, industry, tolerance, region ("sheet metal fabrication stainless steel," "wire harness assembly automotive"). The [keyword research](/news/multilingual-keyword-research-guide/) maps these queries; the site structure should mirror them one-to-one. Each page then becomes the definitive answer to its query cluster — which is also exactly what the [content hub architecture](/news/content-hub-seo-architecture/) needs as its commercial core.

## The anatomy of a service page that converts

Ranking brings the visit; structure wins the inquiry. The proven skeleton, top to bottom:

1. **H1: the capability, plainly stated.** "Custom CNC Machining for Aluminum Enclosures" — the query language, not marketing language. Clever headlines ("Precision Engineered Solutions") rank for nothing and say nothing.
2. **Opening: who this is for and what it covers, in three sentences.** The buyer confirms "yes, this is the page for my problem" within five seconds — the same five-second test as the [homepage first impression](/news/b2b-homepage-first-impression/).
3. **Capability specifics: the engineer's checklist.** Materials handled, size/tolerance ranges, equipment list, certifications, minimum and maximum volumes, industries served. This section does double duty: it's the content Google's quality systems reward (specific, expert, verifiable), and it's what the engineer copies into their supplier comparison sheet. Vague capability claims are why buyers bounce; specifics are why they stay.
4. **Proof.** Photos of actual parts produced, the machines that produce them, inspection reports, certifications — the [trust signals](/news/b2b-website-trust-signals/) deployed where the buying decision actually happens. One real photo of a finished part beats a paragraph of adjectives.
5. **Process: how working with you goes.** Inquiry → quotation → sampling → production → delivery, with realistic timelines. This answers the buyer's next question ("how would this actually work?") before they have to ask, and quietly filters out mismatched prospects.
6. **FAQ: three to five real questions.** MOQ, lead time, file formats accepted, sample policy, payment terms. Real questions from your sales inbox — they capture long-tail searches, pre-answer objections, and qualify for FAQ [structured data](/news/schema-markup-manufacturer-websites/).
7. **The CTA matched to buyer readiness.** Not just "contact us" — the [RFQ flow](/news/rfq-form-design-export-websites/): "Send your drawing for a 48-hour quotation," with the form that accepts files. Engineers attach STEP files; a form without upload loses them.

## The supporting structure around the page

A service page doesn't work alone:

- **A services index that routes, not summarizes.** The parent page lists capabilities with one-line descriptions and links — a directory, not a document. Its job is navigation and crawl paths.
- **Case studies as the proof layer.** "How we machined 40,000 enclosures for a German automation client" — linked from the service page, linking back. Case studies carry the specifics service pages can't (client context, volumes, outcomes) and rank for application-level queries.
- **Blog content feeding the commercial pages.** Selection guides, material comparisons, tolerance explainers — the informational layer of the hub, internally linking up to the service page with descriptive anchors. This is how a new service page earns authority before it has backlinks of its own.
- **Multilingual parity.** Every service page in the languages of your markets — the [language decision](/news/how-many-languages-export-website/) applied to commercial pages first, because a German buyer searches in German even when they read English fine. Proper [hreflang wiring](/news/hreflang-mistakes-multilingual-b2b/) so each market gets its own language version in results.

## The mistakes that sink service pages

- **The mega-page.** All capabilities on one URL (the failure this article opened with). If your services index scrolls forever, split it.
- **Thin pages.** A heading, two sentences, a stock photo. Google's helpful-content systems and human buyers reach the same verdict: nothing here. If a capability is real, it has specifics worth writing; if you can't fill a page, fold it into the parent as a bullet.
- **Duplicate boilerplate.** The same three paragraphs re-skinned for six services, with the keyword swapped. Templated sameness is visible to both rankings and readers — each page needs its own specifics.
- **Marketing voice over engineering voice.** "World-class solutions provider" versus "5-axis machining, ±0.01mm, aluminum and stainless, prototype to 50k units." The [English copywriting rules](/news/english-copywriting-manufacturer-websites/) apply double on service pages: buyers read these pages with a checklist, not with feelings.
- **Orphan pages.** A service page linked from nowhere but the sitemap. It belongs in the main navigation, the index page, the footer, and the internal links of related content — or it will rank for nothing regardless of quality.
