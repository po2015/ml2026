Two companies send the same manual to the same localization vendor. One gets an accurate quote in a day and a smooth project. The other gets a vague estimate, three rounds of questions, a mid-project price revision, and a delivery that misses the launch date. The difference is almost never the vendor. It is the brief.

Localization vendors price and plan based on uncertainty. A vague brief forces them to either pad the quote or gamble — and when the gamble fails, the cost lands back on you as delays and rework. A good brief removes uncertainty, and you get the savings.

## What a localization brief must contain

A complete brief fits on one or two pages and answers eight questions:

1. **What is the content?** Document type (manual, brochure, website, video, course), subject matter, and approximate volume — word count for text, minutes for video, pages for DTP.
2. **What files do you have?** This is the question vendors ask first and clients answer worst. "We have a PDF" is not an answer — is it an editable PDF or a scan? Do you have the source files (InDesign, Word, FrameMaker, PPT)? Source files routinely cut DTP costs by a third.
3. **Which languages?** Be specific about variants: Brazilian Portuguese or European? Latin American Spanish or Castilian? Simplified or Traditional Chinese?
4. **What is it for?** A regulatory submission, a sales brochure, and internal training have different quality bars and different workflows. Purpose determines process.
5. **Who is the audience?** Engineers, procurement managers, end consumers, regulators — tone and terminology choices follow the reader.
6. **What reference materials exist?** Previous translations, glossaries, style guides, brand terminology, competitor materials you like. Every reference asset reduces both cost and risk.
7. **What is the real deadline?** Not the aspirational one. If the trade show is fixed but the brochure could ship a week earlier in digital form, say so — sequencing options appear.
8. **Who approves?** Name the person who answers terminology questions and signs off on final files, and their availability. Projects stall on silent approvers more than on translation itself.

## The brief template

Copy this structure into your next request for quote:

| Section | Example entry |
|---|---|
| Content type | Product installation manual, technical |
| Volume | ~12,000 words, 45 pages, 30 diagrams |
| Source files | InDesign 2024 package + linked images + PDF for reference |
| Target languages | German, Spanish (LatAm), Arabic |
| Purpose | Shipping with product; must match regulatory declarations |
| Audience | Certified installers, technical distributors |
| Reference assets | EN glossary (200 terms), previous edition DE translation, brand style guide |
| Deadline | Final files by March 15; quotes by February 20 |
| Approval contact | Jane Wu, Product Documentation Lead, replies within 24h |
| Deliverables | Print-ready PDF + packaged source files per language |

## Briefing mistakes that cost you money

- **Quoting from a PDF alone.** If you send a flattened PDF for a document that will go to DTP, the vendor quotes extraction and reconstruction. Mention source files even if you cannot share them yet — the price difference is material.
- **Hiding the deadline.** Some buyers keep the real deadline secret, fearing rush pricing. The result is worse: the vendor plans capacity blind, and your "surprise" urgency becomes everyone's emergency. Vendors charge less for planned fast work than for unplanned panic work.
- **No glossary, but strong opinions.** If you will reject translations over terminology, provide the terminology up front. "We'll know it when we see it" is the most expensive sentence in localization.
- **Briefing five vendors identically, then comparing only price.** Briefs enable comparison — but compare scope: does the quote include DTP, linguistic QA, one revision round, terminology management? The cheapest line item often excludes the stages that determine whether the output is usable.
