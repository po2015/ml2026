A buyer walks your booth at Hannover Messe or the Canton Fair, likes the machine, scans the QR code on your banner — and lands on your homepage, where nothing mentions the show, the product she just touched is three clicks deep, and the contact form asks for her life story. She closes the tab. That QR scan was the highest-intent visit your site will get all quarter, and it died of genericness. Trade show landing pages are unglamorous, cheap, and routinely the best-converting pages on a manufacturer's site. Here is the playbook, before your next show.

## Why a dedicated page beats the homepage

Show visitors are a distinct audience in a distinct state: they've *met* you, they have limited time (browsing between booths, on hotel Wi-Fi), and they arrive with context — the show, your booth, possibly a conversation. Sending them to a generic homepage forces them to rebuild that context themselves. A dedicated page converts because it *continues the conversation instead of restarting it*. It also gives you something the homepage never can: clean attribution. Every visit, download, and inquiry from that URL came from the show — which finally lets you calculate what the booth actually returned, the measurement discipline from our [GA4 guide](/news/ga4-export-website-metrics/).

## The anatomy of a page that converts

Everything on the page earns its place by serving a buyer who was just at your booth:

1. **Show-matched hero.** Show name, dates, your booth number, and a photo of *your actual booth* — instant confirmation they're in the right place. "Visiting us at Canton Fair 2027? Hall 9.2, Booth D14."
2. **The products that were at the booth.** Not your full catalog — the 3–5 machines physically on display, with the same photos and specs the buyer just saw. Match the mental picture; the [product page structure](/news/product-catalog-website-inquiry-structure/) applies underneath.
3. **The show offer, if you have one.** Show-special pricing, free samples, a DFM review offer — a reason to act this week, not "someday."
4. **Downloads for the suitcase.** PDF datasheets and catalogs — buyers collect documentation at shows to justify the trip internally; a page that hands them the full set in one click saves them carrying paper and gets you the download event.
5. **A named human with direct contact.** The person they met, photo, WhatsApp/WeChat/email. Not a form alone — [trust-signal logic](/news/b2b-website-trust-signals/) says the named human outperforms, and at a show they *already met* the human.
6. **A short, show-specific inquiry form.** "Which product did you see at our booth?" pre-tagged with show and booth context — per the [RFQ form playbook](/news/rfq-form-design-export-websites/), five fields maximum.
7. **A booking link for post-show calls** if your sales team uses one — the buyer's calendar fills fast after a show; being in it is half the battle.

## The multilingual dimension

International shows mean international badge scans. The rule: **the landing page speaks the languages of the show's buyer mix.** Canton Fair and most global shows: English is the floor, plus your two or three target-market languages per the show's actual visitor data. A German buyer scanning your QR at Hannover and landing on a German page with show-specific content is a small touch with outsized effect — it signals exactly the [market commitment](/news/german-market-b2b-buyer-expectations/) that wins those buyers. The page doesn't need your whole site translated; it needs *this one page* in the right languages, linked from your language switcher or geo-aware redirect.

## Timing and promotion

- **Publish 2–3 weeks before the show.** Print the QR code on booth graphics, badge lanyards, business cards, and giveaway inserts. One URL, everywhere: `yoursite.com/cantonfair2027/`.
- **Drive pre-show traffic.** Email your prospect list with "meet us at booth D14" linking the page; post it on LinkedIn; add it to your email signature. Pre-booked meetings are the highest-value show outcome, and the page is where they start.
- **Keep it live after the show** — with a one-line update ("Thanks for visiting — the show offer is extended through May 15"). Show leads take weeks to mature; the page that 404s after teardown wastes them. Next year, clone and update; the URLs and measurement history accumulate.
- **Retarget the scanners.** Visitors who scanned but didn't inquire are a warm audience for follow-up email or ads — but only if you tagged the traffic properly.

## The measurement loop

Track three numbers per show: QR-scan sessions, engagement (datasheet downloads, time on page), and inquiries tagged to the show URL. After two or three shows you can finally answer the question every CFO asks — *what does a show actually return?* — with per-show cost per qualified inquiry. Most manufacturers discover one show outperforms the rest by 3×, and reallocate. None of that is possible while QR codes point at the homepage.
