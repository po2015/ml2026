A marketing team prices dubbing for their video library: 40 product videos, 4 target languages. All-human studio recording comes back at a number that kills the project. They pivot to all-AI voices — fast and cheap — and the first German video pronounces the product name like a breakfast cereal, while the emotional customer-testimonial segment sounds like a weather report. Both failures were avoidable, because the question was framed wrong. "AI or human?" is a false binary; the working question is "which voice, for which segment, of which video?" Hybrid dubbing workflows — human and AI voices mixed deliberately within one program, often within one video — deliver 70–80% of all-human quality at a fraction of the cost. Here's how the routing actually works.

## Route by video tier first

The coarsest split happens at the library level. Rank your videos into tiers:

| Tier | Content type | Voice decision |
|---|---|---|
| **Tier 1** | Brand films, flagship product launches, customer testimonials, ads | **Human.** Emotional range and brand voice are the product here |
| **Tier 2** | Product demos, explainer videos, sales-enablement content | **Hybrid.** Human for on-camera talent sync and emphasis passages; AI for descriptive stretches |
| **Tier 3** | Support/tutorials, update announcements, internal training, FAQ videos | **AI**, human-polished. High volume, short shelf life, informational tone |

The logic: human voices are bought where *persuasion* lives, AI where *information* lives. A [product demo video](/news/product-demo-video-localization/) sits exactly on the boundary — which is why Tier 2 is where the real hybrid craft happens.

## Route by segment within a video

Within a single Tier 2 video, segments differ in stakes. A typical split:

- **On-camera speaker lip-sync passages** → human voice matching the speaker's energy, or AI with careful timing work. (For true close-up lip-sync, compare [voiceover vs. dubbing vs. subtitles](/news/voiceover-vs-dubbing-vs-subtitles/) economics first — sometimes the answer is not to dub the on-camera parts at all.)
- **Screen-capture narration / B-roll description** → AI voice. Flat, informational, specs-heavy content is AI's home turf; nobody needs a trained actor to say "the torque range is 40 to 180 Newton-metres."
- **The emotional beats** — customer quotes, the problem-story opening, the closing call-to-action → human. These are the 20 seconds viewers remember.
- **Legal/safety lines** (warnings, compliance statements) → whichever voice, but human *reviewed* word-for-word. A misrendered safety instruction is a liability, not a style issue — the same logic as [compliance training translation](/news/compliance-training-translation-accuracy/).

Mixed in one timeline, the listener's ear follows the *consistency of quality*, not the consistency of source. The junctions matter more than the choices: loudness-match the segments (target the same LUFS), keep room tone consistent under both, and don't cut from a warm human close-mic to a sterile AI render mid-paragraph.

## The production pipeline that makes hybrid work

1. **Script locked before any voice is generated.** AI voices make iteration cheap — which tempts teams into translating sloppy scripts. The [voiceover script preparation](/news/voiceover-script-preparation/) discipline (timed, segmented, pronunciation-noted) applies *more* in hybrid, because two production tracks consume the same script.
2. **Pronunciation lexicon per language.** Your product names, model numbers, and brand terms — the same assets as your [termbase](/news/terminology-management-termbase/) — fed to the AI engine as phonetic overrides and given to human talent as a pronunciation guide. This one step removes the most embarrassing AI failures.
3. **AI first pass, human review.** Generate all AI segments, then have a native speaker review *audio*, not script — mispronunciations and wrong emphasis are audible, not visible. Flagged segments get regenerated with adjusted markup or escalated to a human retake.
4. **Human sessions batched.** All human segments across all videos recorded in one session per language — batching is where studio economics work. This is also where [remote recording](/news/remote-voiceover-recording-guide/) cuts cost: broadcast-quality home studios beat studio-day rates for short segments.
5. **One mix engineer owns the seams.** Loudness, EQ character, and pacing matched across sources; the deliverable should never sound like two workflows stapled together.

## Where hybrid breaks down

Honest limits: **dialogue-heavy content** (multi-speaker dramas, interview formats) mixes poorly — voice-source switches between conversational partners are jarring; go all-human. **Character/brand mascots** must be one voice, always human, always the same actor; AI clones of a brand voice are a legal and consistency minefield. And **highly inflected languages** expose AI stress-pattern errors more than English does — budget extra review passes for languages like Czech or Finnish, and always validate with native ears rather than assuming the English AI quality transfers (the [AI vs. human voice](/news/ai-dubbing-vs-human-voice/) trade-offs shift per language pair).

## The cost picture, honestly

For a typical exporter library — say 40 videos averaging 4 minutes, 4 languages: all-human dubbing runs to five figures per language; all-AI to low four; a sensible hybrid (Tier 1 human, Tier 2 split, Tier 3 AI) lands around a quarter of the all-human cost while keeping every customer-facing persuasion moment in a real voice. The savings compound on updates: when the product spec changes, you regenerate two AI sentences instead of rebooking a studio — the same maintenance logic as [batch-updating course libraries](/news/batch-video-localization-course-libraries/).
