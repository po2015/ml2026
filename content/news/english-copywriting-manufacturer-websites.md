A procurement manager in Ohio opens two supplier websites. The first says: "We are a leading professional manufacturer devoted to excellence with perfect quality and best service." The second says: "We machine aluminum housings for medical device OEMs — tolerances to ±0.01 mm, ISO 13485 certified, 15-day standard lead time." One of these companies gets the RFQ. It is not the first one.

Most manufacturer websites are translated from Chinese marketing conventions, where expansive, relationship-oriented language signals ambition. Anglo B2B buyers read the same language as evasion: if you will not say specifically what you do, you probably cannot do it. This article covers the copywriting patterns that build credibility with Western industrial buyers.

## The core principle: specifics are the persuasion

Western B2B copy works on evidence density. Every claim needs a number, a name, or a verifiable fact attached. Compare:

| Chinese-convention English | What buyers want |
|---|---|
| "Advanced production equipment" | "14 CNC machining centers (DMG MORI, Haas), newest installed 2024" |
| "Strict quality control system" | "ISO 9001:2015 certified; CMM inspection on 100% of medical parts" |
| "Rich export experience" | "Exporting since 2011; 60% of output to Germany and the US" |
| "Widely used in many industries" | "Current applications: MRI housings, surgical robot arms, lab automation" |
| "Competitive price with high quality" | Delete — every supplier says this; it carries zero information |

The right column is shorter, easier to write, and infinitely more convincing. Buyers are scanning for the three facts that determine whether you make their shortlist: **capability** (can you make my part?), **credibility** (can I verify you?), and **capacity** (can you handle my volume?).

## The five sections where copy wins or loses

### Homepage headline

State what you make and for whom, in one sentence: "Precision CNC machining for medical and aerospace OEMs." Not "Welcome to our website," not a slogan. The headline is the single most-read sentence on your site.

### About page

Western buyers read About pages to assess risk, not to be inspired. Lead with facts: founded year, employee count, factory area, equipment list, certifications, export markets. Founder story and company culture belong at the bottom, briefly. The Chinese convention of leading with the chairman's philosophy reads as filler to a buyer checking whether you will exist in three years.

### Product pages

Structure every product page identically: specifications table first (dimensions, materials, tolerances, standards), then application examples, then customization options. Buyers compare tables across suppliers; marketing prose gets skipped entirely.

### Case studies

The highest-converting content a manufacturer can publish. Structure: customer type and industry (anonymized is fine — "a Tier-1 automotive supplier in Michigan"), the problem, what you delivered, and measurable outcome. Two solid case studies outperform twenty product photos.

### Contact page

Every field of friction costs inquiries. Offer a direct email to a named person, response time commitment ("we reply within 24 hours"), and WhatsApp for markets that use it. "Please fill in the form and we will contact you" with no other option filters out exactly the senior buyers you want.

## Phrases to delete from your website today

- "Leading manufacturer" / "world-class" / "first-class" — unverifiable superlatives; show evidence instead
- "Perfect quality" — invites skepticism; state tolerances and defect rates instead
- "Sincerely welcome friends from all over the world" — pure filler in Anglo business culture
- "We will try our best" — signals uncertainty; state what you commit to
- Chinglish constructions: "Warmly welcome to visit our factory," "satisfy your demand," "co-create brilliant future"

These are not grammar problems — they are convention problems. Each one tells a native-English buyer the site was translated, not written, and by extension that the company may not be set up for export business.

## Voice and mechanics

- **Short sentences.** 15–20 words average. Engineering buyers skim; long subordinated sentences lose them.
- **Active voice.** "We inspect every part" beats "Every part is inspected by us."
- **Second person where natural.** "You get a dedicated project engineer" beats "Customers will receive..."
- **Standard American or British English — pick one and be consistent.** Mixed spellings (aluminum/aluminium, color/colour) on the same page look unedited.
- **No exclamation marks in B2B copy.** Ever.

## The practical path

Most manufacturers do not need a copywriter on staff; they need their existing content rebuilt once, properly:

1. **Extract the facts**: equipment list, certifications with numbers, export history, reference industries, lead times.
2. **Rewrite the core pages** (homepage, about, top 3 product pages, contact) with a native B2B copywriter who understands manufacturing.
3. **Apply the templates**: product page structure, case study structure, specification table format.
4. **Translate back, not forward**: your Chinese site can then adopt the evidence-dense style too — it works there as well.
