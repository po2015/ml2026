A global sales academy rolls out its flagship scenario course — branching role-plays where learners handle an angry customer, negotiate a discount, and push back on an unrealistic deadline. US completion rates: excellent. The Japan rollout: polite confusion. Learners report the "difficult customer" behaves so aggressively that no real customer would act that way, the answer marked correct — calmly refusing the discount — would damage the relationship, and the joke in the feedback screen reads as sarcasm toward the learner. The translation was perfect. The *scenario* wasn't. Scenario-based training packs more cultural assumption per screen than any other e-learning format, and localizing it means adapting behavior, not just language.

## Why scenarios break where slides don't

A product-knowledge slide states facts; facts travel. A scenario simulates a social interaction, and social interactions are locally defined. The embedded assumptions that break in transit:

- **Power distance.** A scenario where a junior employee challenges a manager's decision plays as empowering in the US or Netherlands — and as a firing offense in high power-distance cultures. The *learning objective* (speak up about safety) may be identical; the *behavior modeled* must change.
- **Feedback directness.** Dutch and German business culture rewards blunt disagreement; Japanese and many Asian contexts route disagreement indirectly. A "correct answer" that says "Tell the client their timeline is impossible" teaches the right skill in one market and relationship damage in another.
- **Customer behavior.** What counts as an "angry customer" — volume, directness, what's negotiable — varies enormously. Scenarios must model the customer the learner will actually meet.
- **Humor, idioms, and informality.** Jokes in feedback screens, baseball metaphors, first-name-basis dialogue with executives — each is a small cultural tell that the course wasn't made *for* this learner.

This is why [scenario work belongs in the cultural layer](/news/elearning-localization-global-audiences/) of a localization program, not the linguistic one — and why the review process differs from standard [course translation](/news/articulate-storyline-translation-workflow/).

## What to adapt — the four layers

Adapt in this order; each layer is cheaper than the one below it:

1. **Surface signals (always).** Names, locations, currencies, date formats, honorifics. "Mike from Dallas" becomes locally plausible characters. Cheap, mechanical, mandatory — the same hygiene as [format localization](/news/date-currency-format-localization/) and [image choices](/news/image-icon-color-localization/).
2. **Dialogue register (always).** Formality levels, greetings, how directly requests are phrased. In many languages this is structural — Japanese keigo or German Sie/du choices change every line of dialogue, not just word choice. Native copywriters, not translators, own this layer.
3. **Interaction logic (usually).** What the "best" response is. Keep the learning objective fixed; re-plot the path to it. Objective: *decline an unreasonable discount without losing the deal.* US path: polite direct refusal with justification. Adapted path for indirect-communication markets: acknowledge the request, explore the underlying need, offer alternative value. Same skill, culturally valid route.
4. **Scenario premise (sometimes).** Occasionally the whole situation doesn't exist locally — a returns-policy scenario in a market where returns don't work that way; a tipping joke in a no-tipping culture. Replace the premise with a local equivalent that drills the same objective.

## A worked example

One scenario, three adaptations:

| Element | US original | Germany | Japan | Gulf |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Customer complaint style | Loud, threatens to leave | Blunt, cites the contract clause | Apologetic but firm, escalates indirectly | Relationship-framed, expects personal attention |
| "Correct" first move | Acknowledge + offer solution immediately | Acknowledge + verify facts precisely | Apologize sincerely + investigate before promising | Acknowledge personally + involve a senior name |
| Feedback tone for wrong answer | Playful sarcasm | Direct correction | Encouraging, face-saving | Respectful, formal |
| Names/settings | Mike / Dallas showroom | Herr Becker / Munich office | Tanaka-san / Osaka HQ | Mr. Al-Rashid / Riyadh branch |

Notice what doesn't change: the branching structure, the scoring, the learning objective, the number of decision points. The skeleton is global; the flesh is local.

## What to protect

Adaptation has limits — cross them and you've built a different course per market, with no comparability:

- **Learning objectives and assessment validity.** If the Germany version teaches "verify facts" and the US version teaches "respond fast," your completion data means nothing across regions. Objectives stay fixed in writing before adaptation starts; any change to *what's being taught* is a global course decision, not a local one. This matters doubly for [assessed content](/news/quiz-assessment-localization-pitfalls/), where the "right answer" must be defensible in every market.
- **The branching architecture.** Decision points, consequences, and scoring logic stay identical — it's what lets you maintain the course in [one authoring project](/news/articulate-storyline-translation-workflow/) instead of five.
- **Compliance content.** Safety and legal scenarios adapt *tone*, never *substance*. A lockout-tagout scenario teaches the same steps everywhere.

## The review process that catches this

Standard linguistic review won't catch cultural misfires — reviewers check language, and the language is fine. What works:

1. **Cultural review before translation.** An in-country reviewer (sales lead, distributor trainer) reads the *English* scenario and flags premise and interaction-logic issues. Fixing layer 3–4 problems in English costs a fraction of fixing them in five translated builds.
2. **Adaptation notes in the translation kit.** For each scenario: what's sacred (objective, structure), what's free (names, register, examples). Without this, translators either freeze and translate literally — preserving the cultural bugs — or freelance silently, breaking comparability.
3. **Native pilot with real learners.** Three to five target-market learners per language, thinking aloud through the scenarios. They find the "no customer talks like that" moments that every document review misses. Budget it into the [project cost model](/news/elearning-localization-cost-budget/) — it's small and it's the highest-signal QA you can buy.
