A procurement engineer shortlists three suppliers for a hydraulic component. All three websites list similar specs. One shows crisp studio shots from five angles, a cutaway view, and photos of the production line where the part is made. The second has decent product shots but nothing else. The third — your company — shows the product on a oily bench with a tape measure for scale, photographed on a phone. The engineer doesn't consciously think "bad photos"; they think "I can picture working with the first one." Industrial product photography isn't decoration — it's the closest thing to a factory visit you can give a buyer 8,000 kilometers away, and it does more [trust-building work](/news/b2b-website-trust-signals/) than any paragraph of copy. Here's what to shoot and how.

## What buyers are actually looking for in photos

Nobody admits to judging suppliers by photography, but purchasing behavior says otherwise. Buyers scan images for specific evidence:

- **Manufacturing reality.** Is there an actual factory? Clean production-line photos, machinery, workers in uniform, organized assembly stations. This answers the "is this a real manufacturer or a trading company" question that sits behind every [B2B first impression](/news/b2b-homepage-first-impression/).
- **Quality discipline.** Inspection stations, testing equipment, measurement tools in use, certificates being checked. A photo of a coordinate measuring machine in action says more about QC than the word "strict" ever will.
- **Product truth.** Sharp, well-lit product shots that match the spec sheet — real materials, real finish, real weld seams. Buyers cross-check photos against drawings; inconsistencies trigger questions.
- **Scale and capability.** Warehouse shots, container loading, the range of machines on the floor. These answer "can they handle my volume" without a word.

The corollary: stock photography is actively harmful. Buyers have seen the same shaking-hands photo on forty supplier sites, and reverse-image search exists. One genuine, slightly imperfect photo of your actual shop floor beats a library of stock.

## The shot list every manufacturer needs

Organize the shoot around the website's needs, not the other way around. The core list:

| Shot type | Purpose | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| Hero product shots | Clean background, multiple angles, consistent lighting across the whole catalog | Product pages, [catalog structure](/news/product-catalog-website-inquiry-structure/) |
| Detail close-ups | Surface finish, connectors, labels, serial plates — the parts engineers zoom into | Product pages |
| Scale references | Product in hand, next to a person, on a pallet — size is impossible to judge from specs alone | Product pages |
| In-application shots | The product installed, operating, in its working environment | Product pages, case studies |
| Production line | Machines running, assembly stations, the flow of work | About, capability pages |
| QC and testing | Inspection in progress, test rigs, lab equipment | About, quality pages |
| Packaging and logistics | Export packing, crate labeling, container loading — proof you ship internationally | About, FAQ |
| People | Team at work, engineers at screens, real faces | About, contact — the human layer of trust |

Notice how many of these aren't product shots at all. The product catalog proves what you make; the factory and people photos prove who you are — and on a manufacturer's website, the second sale happens first.

## Shooting it without a five-figure budget

Professional industrial photographers exist and are worth it for hero shots. But a working catalog library is achievable in-house with discipline:

1. **Light is 80% of the job.** A daylight-balanced LED panel pair (a few hundred dollars) beats any camera upgrade. Overhead factory fluorescents produce the green-yellow cast that reads as "low quality" — turn them off for product shots, or shoot near a loading-bay door for natural light.
2. **Background discipline.** One consistent sweep — white or light gray seamless paper for small parts, a clean dedicated corner for large equipment. Consistency across 200 SKUs is what makes a catalog look like a catalog; shoot every product against the same background from the same angles, and the grid page assembles itself.
3. **Modern phones are enough for most shots.** A recent flagship phone on a $50 tripod, with HDR off and the lens wiped, produces web-ready images. The failures that matter are lighting, background, and stability — not sensor size.
4. **Shoot raw volume, curate later.** Photograph everything in one dedicated week: every product from six angles, every machine, every station. Selection is cheaper than re-shooting.
5. **Clean before you shoot.** Wipe the product, clear the bench, coil the cables. Thirty seconds of housekeeping per shot is the difference between "factory" and "workshop."

One warning on retouching: correct exposure and white balance freely, but never "improve" the product itself — removing scratches, beautifying welds, or color-shifting finishes. Engineers will receive the actual product; photos that overpromise turn into disputes, returns, and lost accounts. Authenticity is the asset.

## The web side: photos that don't sink your site

Great photography can still damage the site if it's served badly:

- **Compress aggressively.** A 4MB straight-from-camera JPEG on every product card destroys [page speed](/news/export-website-speed-cdn-performance/) — and [Core Web Vitals](/news/core-web-vitals-b2b-exporters/) with it. Export at the dimensions actually displayed, use WebP, target under 200KB per image.
- **Name files for search.** `hydraulic-valve-pvg32-flange.jpg`, not `IMG_4521.jpg` — file names and alt text are how product images surface in Google Images, a real discovery channel for engineers.
- **Alt text for every image.** Describe what's visible: "PVG-32 hydraulic valve, flange side view." Accessibility, SEO, and a fallback when images fail to load — one field, three jobs.
- **Mind the localization layer.** Photos containing text — control panels, labels, packaging with language — need either language-neutral staging or [per-market variants](/news/image-icon-color-localization/), the same way [infographics and charts](/news/infographic-chart-localization/) do. A photo of your product labeled in Chinese on the German site quietly undermines the "international supplier" message.

## The maintenance rhythm

Photography decays: products get revised, the factory gets new equipment, the team photo includes people who left two years ago. Put it on the [website maintenance plan](/news/website-maintenance-plan-exporters/): new products photographed at launch against the standard background, facility shots refreshed annually, and a standing "photo needed" flag in the [catalog workflow](/news/oem-product-catalog-website-guide/) so no SKU ships to the site with a placeholder. Companies moving [from marketplace storefronts to owned sites](/news/alibaba-to-owned-website-migration/) feel this most — the marketplace hid weak photography behind a uniform template; your own site exposes it.
