Europe looks like one market on a trade map and behaves like twenty-seven on the ground. An exporter's first instinct — "translate the site into European" — collides with reality: 24 official EU languages, wildly different English tolerance by country, and purchasing power concentrated in a handful of economies. The good news: B2B buying power in Europe is far more concentrated than the language count suggests. A disciplined five-language build covers the overwhelming majority of EU purchasing; the trick is knowing which five, in which order, and where English alone is genuinely enough.

## The buying-power map, not the population map

Prioritize by B2B purchasing weight — GDP, industrial base, and your sector's demand — not by speaker counts:

| Language | Native speakers (EU) | B2B weight | English tolerance in B2B |
|---|---|---|---|
| German | ~90M (DE/AT + CH) | Very high — largest industrial economy | **Low** — buyers expect German |
| French | ~70M (FR/BE/LU) | High — second economy, strong industry | **Low-medium** — French expected |
| Italian | ~60M | High — manufacturing powerhouse | **Medium-low** |
| Spanish | ~48M (ES) | Medium-high (+ LatAm bonus reach) | **Medium** |
| Polish | ~40M | Medium-high, fast-growing industry | **Medium** |
| Dutch | ~24M (NL/BE) | High per-capita, trade hub | **High** — English widely accepted |
| Swedish/Danish/Finnish | ~20M combined | High per-capita | **Very high** — English is fine |
| Czech/Romanian/Hungarian… | ~50M combined | Medium, growing | **Medium-high** |

The pattern: English tolerance tracks neither wealth nor size alone. Scandinavia and the Netherlands buy comfortably in English; Germany — Europe's biggest industrial buyer — famously does not. This mirrors what we detailed for the [German market](/news/german-market-b2b-buyer-expectations/): a German-language site is not a courtesy there, it's a credibility threshold.

## The coverage math

For industrial B2B, a realistic tiering:

- **Tier 1 — English + German.** Covers the DACH industrial heartland plus every English-tolerant market (Nordics, Benelux, CEE procurement teams that work in English). For most machinery and component exporters, this pair already reaches roughly two-thirds of addressable EU purchasing.
- **Tier 2 — + French + Italian + Spanish.** Five languages total now cover the large economies where local language materially moves conversion. You're at or near the "90% of realistic demand" line.
- **Tier 3 — situational.** Polish (if CEE manufacturing supply chains are your play), Dutch (if Benelux distribution is strategic), Portuguese (usually via the [Latin-American-adjacent Spanish/Portuguese decision](/news/latin-america-spanish-variants/) — and remember Spain's Spanish differs from [Latin American variants](/news/spanish-seo-latin-america-spain/)).

Beyond Tier 3, returns drop steeply: the remaining languages each address small B2B slices that mostly *also* work in English or German. Translate for them when a distributor deal justifies it, not before.

## Three rules that save budget

1. **German first, always.** If you do one European language, it's German. Biggest industrial economy, lowest English tolerance, and the bonus reach into Austria and Switzerland. Skipping German to do French first because "France is bigger by population" is the classic mistake — population isn't purchasing behavior.
2. **English-tolerant markets need *proof*, not translation.** For Scandinavia and the Netherlands, spend the budget on an excellent English site — deep specs, strong [trust signals](/news/b2b-website-trust-signals/), local case references — rather than on translation those buyers don't require. Revisit only if analytics show underperformance.
3. **Spanish and Portuguese decisions are global, not European.** Your Spanish build serves Spain *and* opens Latin America; decide the variant strategy once ([the differences matter](/news/latin-america-spanish-variants/)), and consider whether Brazil belongs in the same phase.

## The cost side of the decision

Each language is a permanent commitment, not a one-time project: new products, news, and [every future page](/news/how-many-languages-export-website/) multiply by your language count. Budget realistically:

- **Initial build**: the site core — homepage, company, top product categories, [RFQ path](/news/rfq-form-design-export-websites/) — typically 40–80 pages per language.
- **Steady state**: 15–25% of the initial volume per year in updates, plus translation memory [driving revision costs down](/news/translation-memory-dtp-efficiency/) over time.
- **The hidden cost is review, not translation.** Each language needs someone who can approve terminology — a distributor, a regional sales lead, or a retained reviewer. Launch languages only as fast as you can staff that function.

This is why the phased rollout beats the big bang: launch German, measure inquiry quality for two quarters, then add French and Italian with the process already debugged. The [localization workflow](/news/website-localization-workflow/) is identical each time; the second language costs roughly 60–70% of the first.

## The 12-month sequence that works

1. **Q1**: English site hardened (it's the hub everything links from) + German launch.
2. **Q2**: German learns — terminology base established, distributor review loop working.
3. **Q3**: French + Italian, reusing the German process and TM infrastructure.
4. **Q4**: Spanish (with the LatAm variant decision made), Polish if CEE is strategic.

By year-end: five languages, ~90% of addressable EU B2B demand covered, and a repeatable machine for language six when a market proves it deserves one.
