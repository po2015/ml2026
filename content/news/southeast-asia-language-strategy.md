Southeast Asia tempts exporters with 680 million consumers and a booming industrial sector, then confronts them with a hard question on day one: which languages do you publish in? The region has no shared language. Bahasa Indonesia and Malay are close cousins; Vietnamese, Thai, Burmese, Khmer, Lao, Tagalog, and a dozen others are not. English proficiency ranges from near-native (Singapore, Philippines) to functional-in-business (Malaysia, Vietnam's cities) to limited (much of Indonesia, Thailand, rural Vietnam). There is no single right answer — but there is a defensible sequence.

## The language landscape in business terms

| Market | Business language reality | Website implication |
|---|---|---|
| Singapore | English is the working language | English-only is fine |
| Philippines | English standard in B2B | English-only is fine |
| Malaysia | English common in business; Bahasa Malaysia for government-adjacent sectors | English works; BM adds reach |
| Indonesia | Bahasa Indonesia expected; English limited outside Jakarta multinationals | Localize — biggest prize in the region |
| Vietnam | Vietnamese expected; English growing in manufacturing/procurement | Localize for scale; English for FDI-adjacent buyers |
| Thailand | Thai expected; English weakest among the big economies | Localize |
| Myanmar/Cambodia/Laos | Local languages; thin B2B web ecosystems | Serve via distributors; low priority |

Two facts sharpen the picture. First, **Indonesia alone is 40% of the region's population** — a Bahasa Indonesia site reaches more people than any other single-language investment. Second, **B2B buying still runs on relationships and messaging apps** (WhatsApp, LINE in Thailand, Zalo in Vietnam) — your website supports the sale more than it closes it, which argues for solid, credible local-language sites over exhaustive ones.

## A defensible sequence

**Tier 1 — English done properly.** Before any translation, your English site must work for non-native readers: shorter sentences, no idioms, specs in tables. Across SEA, the person shortlisting suppliers often reads English well enough — and forwards the link to a boss who doesn't. The English site is your regional floor.

**Tier 2 — Bahasa Indonesia and Vietnamese.** These two add the largest incremental audiences where English genuinely blocks business. Indonesia for its size; Vietnam because its manufacturing boom means procurement managers researching foreign suppliers daily, in Vietnamese.

**Tier 3 — Thai and Bahasa Malaysia.** Add when you have distributor traction. Thai because the market is large and English-poor; BM because the marginal cost is low if you already maintain Bahasa Indonesia (the languages share much vocabulary, though they are *not* interchangeable — treat them as siblings, not duplicates).

**Not on the list:** Tagalog (English covers Philippine B2B), Burmese/Khmer/Lao (distributor-served markets for most exporters).

## What "localizing for SEA" actually involves

- **It's not just language.** Indonesian and Vietnamese buyers expect local contact paths — a local phone number or WhatsApp, a local distributor name, prices in IDR/VND or USD with clear Incoterms. The pattern echoes what we wrote about [Latin America](/news/latin-america-spanish-variants/): the website's job is to make the first human conversation easy.
- **Formality registers matter more than in Europe.** Thai and Vietnamese encode politeness levels in pronouns and particles; marketing copy translated too casually reads as disrespectful, too formally as stiff. Use native copywriters or transcreation for headlines and CTAs even when body text is straight translation.
- **Scripts need font and layout checks.** Thai has no spaces between words (line-breaking needs proper algorithms), Vietnamese stacks diacritics that cheap fonts render badly — the same [font-pairing discipline](/news/cjk-latin-font-pairing/) as CJK, with different specifics.
- **Mobile-first is not a slogan here.** SEA is among the most mobile-dominant internet regions on earth; if your localized pages aren't fast and readable on a mid-range Android, you built them for your own office, not your buyers. Test like it, per our [homepage audit](/news/b2b-homepage-first-impression/).

## The economics

A focused SEA language rollout — English polished, plus Bahasa Indonesia and Vietnamese — typically costs less than a single European language pair done to the same depth, because regional translation rates are lower. The expensive version is doing eleven languages shallowly: maintenance cost scales with languages, but inquiry value scales with *credibility per market*. Two languages done well beat eight done thinly, the same conclusion we reached for [Spanish-speaking markets](/news/spanish-seo-latin-america-spain/).
