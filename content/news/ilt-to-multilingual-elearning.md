A manufacturer runs excellent distributor training: two days in a classroom, a senior engineer presenting, hands-on time with the products, questions answered on the spot. Distributors who attend sell measurably better. The problem is arithmetic — 40 countries, 200 distributors, 3 trainers. The obvious conversion move is to record the presentation and put it online with translated subtitles. Six months later the completion rate is 11%, and the distributors who "completed" it can't answer basic product questions. The classroom course didn't fail to convert; it was never *converted* — it was documented. Instructor-led training (ILT) and e-learning teach differently, and the gap widens with every language you add. Here's the conversion that actually works.

## Why the classroom magic doesn't survive recording

Understand what the classroom was actually providing, because the recording keeps almost none of it:

- **Interaction and pacing control.** The trainer reads the room, slows down, repeats, takes questions. A recorded lecture runs at one speed — too fast for some, boring for others — with no correction loop.
- **Hands-on practice.** Two hours of assembly/disassembly with the actual product becomes... a video of someone else's hands. Psychomotor skills don't transfer through observation alone.
- **Social accountability.** In a classroom, everyone stays to the end. Online, the tab closes at minute 9, and 11% completion is the industry-typical outcome for unconverted lecture recordings.
- **The Q&A safety net.** Every classroom confusion gets resolved live. Online, confusion is terminal — the learner just stops, and there's no one to ask.

E-learning that works replaces each of these *by design*: short modules instead of marathon sessions, interactive checks instead of a trainer's gaze, simulations and guided practice instead of watched hands, and structured knowledge checks instead of live Q&A. That's instructional design work — a conversion project, not a recording project.

## The conversion sequence

**1. Audit the classroom course for what does the teaching.** Separate content (information — converts fine) from method (demonstration, practice, Q&A — must be redesigned). The trainer's slide deck is usually 30% of the actual teaching; interview the trainer for the other 70%: the stories, the common mistakes, the questions every class asks.

**2. Restructure for self-paced learning.** Break the two-day arc into 5–10 minute modules with one objective each. Every module ends with an interaction — a knowledge check, a decision scenario, a "what would you do here" — that replaces the trainer's comprehension check. Classroom exercises convert to simulations, annotated teardown videos with pause-and-predict prompts, or guided worksheets the learner completes with their own unit in hand.

**3. Build the assessment layer from the learning objectives.** The classroom validated understanding by vibes; e-learning must define it: quiz banks per module, a practical assignment where possible, a final assessment with a defensible pass mark — designed from day one with [quiz localization](/news/quiz-assessment-localization-pitfalls/) in mind, since scenario questions and drag-and-drops have their own translation traps.

**4. Produce in the authoring tool, not in video.** Tools like Articulate Storyline or Rise produce structured, updatable, translatable courses (the [Storyline translation workflow](/news/articulate-storyline-translation-workflow/) is mature: export XLIFF, translate, reimport). Video has its place — demonstrations, factory footage — as embedded media inside modules, not as the course. [SCORM or xAPI packaging](/news/scorm-xapi-localization/) decided by your LMS, per the usual criteria.

**5. Pilot in one language, then localize.** Validate completion rates and assessment results with a friendly distributor group before spending on 5 languages. What you fix in the pilot (a confusing simulation, a too-long module) you fix once; discovered after localization, you fix it 6 times.

## The multilingual multipliers

Everything above gets harder per language, in specific ways:

- **Text expansion** breaks layouts built tight in English — design screens with the [expansion headroom](/news/elearning-ui-text-expansion/) from the first storyboard, not as a German-afterthought.
- **Narration costs scale with word count.** A chatty classroom transcript is 30–50% longer than tight e-learning scripts. Tighten *before* translation, or pay for and sit through rambling narration in 5 languages ([voiceover style](/news/elearning-voiceover-narration-styles/) decisions — human vs. [AI voices](/news/ai-dubbing-vs-human-voice/) — belong to the same budget line).
- **Scenarios and examples are cultural content.** A distributor-negotiation scenario written for China reads oddly in Germany; the [scenario localization](/news/scenario-training-cultural-localization/) pass adapts names, settings, and business customs per market.
- **The LMS deployment is per-language work** — enrollments, certificates, completion tracking, per the [deployment checklist](/news/multilingual-lms-deployment/). Budget it, or translated courses sit on a shelf.

## The honest comparison

ILT conversion done properly costs more upfront than "record the trainer" — and delivers what the classroom delivered: distributors who actually learn. The record-and-subtitle route is cheaper in year one and delivers 11% completion forever. And the classroom doesn't have to die: the strongest model for distributors is often **blended** — e-learning for knowledge (localized, scalable), periodic live sessions for practice and relationships (small groups, no travel economics problem). Let each format do what it's good at.
