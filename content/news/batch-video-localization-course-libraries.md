A manufacturer's training team inherits a library: 47 product videos, recorded over four years, ranging from 3-minute feature demos to 40-minute installation walkthroughs. The directive: German, Spanish, Japanese, and Portuguese versions, this year. The project manager starts the obvious way — picks the first video, sends it for translation and dubbing, gets quotes back, and nearly abandons the project: at per-video rates with per-video setup, 47 × 4 comes out to a number finance will never approve. The library isn't unaffordable; it's just being priced as 188 small projects instead of one production line. Batch localization is how video libraries actually get done — the same content, processed as a pipeline, at a fraction of the per-unit cost.

## Why per-video pricing collapses at library scale

Every localization project has fixed costs that don't scale with runtime: glossary setup, voice casting, style decisions, intro/outro graphic adaptation, project management, QA passes. For one video, those are 30–40% of the budget. For a 47-video library done one at a time, you pay them 47 times — *per language*. The batch approach pays them once:

- **One glossary and style guide** governs all 47 videos; terminology consistency across the library comes free instead of requiring 47 alignment meetings.
- **One voice per language** is cast for the whole library (crucial — learners notice when "the instructor" changes voice between modules), booked as a volume engagement at volume rates.
- **One graphic template** handles all intros, titles, and lower-thirds; the [on-screen text adaptation](/news/on-screen-text-video-localization/) becomes a production step, not a design project, per video.
- **One QA protocol** applies across the batch, with reviewers who get faster with every video because the terminology and style decisions are already made.

## The pipeline design

Batch localization works as stages, with all videos flowing through each stage before the next begins:

| Stage | What happens | Batch advantage |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Triage | Inventory, prioritize, decide treatment per video | Spend flows to the videos that matter (see below) |
| 2. Transcription & prep | Source transcripts, timing, glossary extraction | Transcripts batch-processed; glossary built once from all 47 scripts |
| 3. Translation | All scripts translated with shared TM/glossary | [TM leverage](/news/translation-memory-dtp-efficiency/) compounds — product videos repeat the same sentences constantly |
| 4. Voice production | Casting once, [recording in batches](/news/remote-voiceover-recording-guide/), or [AI voices](/news/ai-dubbing-vs-human-voice/) for the appropriate tier | One voice, volume rates, consistent series sound |
| 5. Assembly | Subtitles/dubbing, graphics swap, rendering | Template-driven; rendering is a queue, not a project |
| 6. QA & delivery | Staged review, LMS-ready packaging | Reviewers calibrate once, then accelerate; delivery plugs into the [LMS deployment](/news/multilingual-lms-deployment/) checklist |

The deep secret is in stage 3: product-training libraries are extraordinarily repetitive. "Press and hold the power button for three seconds" appears in dozens of videos. With a shared translation memory, video 30 translates at a fraction of video 1's cost — TM leverage on libraries routinely hits 40–60% by the back half of the batch.

## Triage: not every video deserves the same treatment

The budget lever that makes libraries feasible is differential treatment. Sort the library into tiers before pricing anything:

- **Tier 1 — revenue-critical** (top product demos, the courses customers actually complete): full human dubbing, native voice talent, [lip-sync consideration](/news/lip-sync-vs-voiceover-formats/) where the presenter is prominent, full QA.
- **Tier 2 — core library** (standard product training): AI voices with human review, or [hybrid workflows](/news/ai-dubbing-corporate-training-cost-quality/); subtitle option for markets that prefer it, per the [market format map](/news/subtitles-vs-dubbing-by-market/).
- **Tier 3 — long tail** (legacy products, low-view videos): subtitles only — 90% of the comprehension value at 20% of the cost.
- **Retire**: the 2019 videos for discontinued products that nobody watched in English either. Every library has them; localizing them is pure waste, and triage is the moment to admit it.

A typical 47-video library lands at 8–10 Tier 1, 20–25 Tier 2, the rest Tier 3 and retired — cutting the projected budget by half or more before volume discounts even apply.

## The practical notes that save the schedule

- **Source quality varies across a 4-year library.** Old videos may lack scripts, have burned-in graphics, or exist only as flattened exports. Transcription and graphic-rebuild effort gets estimated per video in triage, or stage 5 stalls on surprises.
- **Batch by language, not by video.** Finish the pipeline for German before starting Spanish — each language team (translator, voice, reviewer) works in one continuous engagement, which is cheaper and faster than interleaving.
- **Version control from day one.** 47 videos × 4 languages × 3 deliverable types (dubbed, subtitled, graphics) is 500+ files; naming conventions and a manifest are the difference between a library and a pile. The discipline pays off again at update time, exactly as in [content sync maintenance](/news/multilingual-content-sync-maintenance/).
- **The business case is cumulative.** Cost per finished minute drops with every video; the executive summary should quote the *blended* rate, not video one's.
