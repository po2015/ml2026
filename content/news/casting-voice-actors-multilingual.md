A marketing manager approves the German voiceover for a product launch video without ever hearing an audition — the vendor's email said "professional native voice, similar profile to the English original." The delivered audio is technically flawless and completely wrong: where the English narrator is measured and senior, the German voice is young and salesy, turning a serious engineering product into something that sounds like an energy-drink spot. Nobody made a mistake exactly; the casting decision was made by default, by someone who'd never been told what the brand sounds like. In multilingual voice production, casting is where brand consistency lives or dies — and it's one of the few stages the client absolutely should not delegate. Here's how to run it.

## Start from the role, not the voice

Clients typically brief "find someone who sounds like our English narrator." Wrong axis. The right starting point: **what role does the voice play in this content?**

- **The authority** (senior engineer, founder): gravitas, measured pace, credibility over charm
- **The guide** (tutorials, [e-learning narration](/news/elearning-voiceover-narration-styles/)): clarity, patience, warmth; stamina for long-form
- **The persuader** ([ads and brand films](/news/marketing-video-localization-ads/)): energy, emotional range, timing
- **The explainer** (product demos): conversational competence — a knowledgeable colleague, not an announcer

A "similar-sounding voice" in another language is a trap: vocal timbre doesn't map across languages, and the same pitch reads younger in German than in English, more formal in Japanese than in Spanish. Cast the *role* in each language, and the voices will feel like the same company even though they sound nothing alike — which is the actual goal.

## The casting brief that prevents defaults

One page, per language, given to every vendor and casting director:

1. **Role and tone**: the function above plus 3 adjectives with reference audio ("measured, senior, unhurried — like the attached English sample, 0:15–0:45").
2. **Demographics with intent**: approximate age read, gender — with *why* (audience expectation, brand persona), not habit. Note where you're flexible.
3. **Accent/dialect specification**: "neutral international Spanish (no regionalisms)," "standard Hochdeutsch," "Brazilian Portuguese, São Paulo neutral" — the single most-skipped line and the source of the most jarring results (a Madrid accent selling to Mexico City reads like a BBC accent selling to Texas; see the [variant problem](/news/brazil-vs-portugal-portuguese-b2b/)).
4. **Technical context**: pacing constraints (must match existing picture timing), style (conversational vs. announcer), and the [delivery specs](/news/audio-loudness-standards-international/) the session must meet.
5. **Usage and budget frame**: media, markets, term — voice pricing depends on usage, and surprises here stall projects after casting.

## The audition: three takes, not a demo reel

Demo reels show an actor's best 30 seconds across every style they've ever done — nearly useless for predicting *your* script. The working audition:

- **Your actual script, 60–90 seconds**, including one technical passage and one brand/persuasion passage. Paid or short unpaid custom auditions are standard practice for professional talent.
- **Two to three candidates per language**, more for flagship projects.
- **Reviewed by two ears**: you (brand fit — does this sound like us?) and a trusted native (credibility — does this sound right *there*? Distributor staff are gold for this, as in [in-context review](/news/in-context-translation-review/)). The native reviewer catches what you can't: a voice that's technically neutral but reads "radio commercial" to local ears.
- **Record the decision**: who was cast, alternates, and why — this becomes your voice roster, so the next project starts from a bench instead of from zero.

## The consistency rules across languages and time

1. **One voice per brand persona per language.** If your German explainer videos have a voice, it's *that* voice — recasting per project fragments the brand audio identity. Build a roster: primary + backup per language.
2. **Voice over voice-match, always.** When the original narrator is unavailable for a sequel, recast the role rather than hiring an impersonator; audiences accept a new voice, they notice a strained imitation.
3. **Lock terms before the session.** Rates, usage rights, buyout terms, revision policy — and for ongoing series, a retainer or first-refusal agreement so your roster stays available.
4. **Budget reality**: professional native voice talent for corporate narration typically runs a few hundred dollars per finished 10 minutes for standard corporate usage, more for broadcast advertising buyouts; [remote-directed sessions](/news/remote-voiceover-recording-guide/) cut studio cost without cutting quality. For high-volume informational content, the [AI-voice hybrid math](/news/hybrid-ai-human-voiceover-workflow/) applies — but casting (choosing *which* voice model and reviewing output) remains a human decision even there.
5. **Keep the sessions directed.** Even great talent misreads a spec sheet's emphasis; a native-speaking director on the session (remote is fine) catches it live instead of in the edit.

Casting is 20% of a voice project's cost and 80% of its brand impact — and in multilingual work it's the stage where "one company, five languages" is either built or quietly abandoned.
