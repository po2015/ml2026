A machinery exporter localizes its flagship corporate video into eight languages. Professional translation, native voiceovers, clean subtitles. Three months later, feedback trickles in: the Middle East distributor asks why the "weekend downtime" scene shows Friday and Saturday as production days; a Japanese partner notes the presenter's self-deprecating joke reads as incompetence; the Brazilian office loved it — except the "OK" hand gesture in the closing montage, which in Brazil is an insult. Every *word* was right. The video still failed in three markets, because translation covered the soundtrack and nobody audited the other two-thirds of what video communicates. This is cultural adaptation: the layer beneath the language.

## The three layers of video meaning

| Layer | What it carries | Localization tool |
|---|---|---|
| Language | Script, narration, on-screen text | Translation, [dubbing/subtitling](/news/subtitles-vs-dubbing-by-market/) |
| Visual culture | Gestures, symbols, colors, dress, settings, who's shown doing what | Cultural audit, re-shoot or re-edit |
| Pragmatics | Humor, formality, directness, persuasion style, pace | Script adaptation, [transcreation](/news/dubbing-script-adaptation-timing/) |

Standard video localization handles layer one and stops. The failures live in layers two and three — and they're invisible to anyone who only reviews the script. You catch them by watching the video as a member of the target culture, which is exactly what a cultural audit is.

## Gestures: the body doesn't translate

Gestures are the classic trap because they're filmed, not written — no translator ever sees them in the script:

- **The thumbs-up** reads as approval in the US and much of Europe, but is offensive in parts of the Middle East, West Africa, and South America.
- **The "OK" circle** means "fine" in the US, "zero" in France, money in Japan — and is an obscenity in Brazil and Turkey.
- **Pointing with one finger** is rude in Malaysia and much of Southeast Asia (use an open hand or thumb); **showing soles of feet** offends in Thailand and Arab markets; the **left hand** for handing objects reads as disrespectful in Muslim and Hindu cultures.
- **Head nods** aren't universal either — the Bulgarian head-sway and the Indian head-wobble both confound outsiders reading "yes/no."

The handling options, in ascending cost: cut the shot (usually possible — gesture footage is rarely load-bearing), replace it in the edit with neutral B-roll, or re-shoot the segment. What's not an option is shipping it and hoping.

## Humor: export at your own risk

Humor is the least portable content in any video, because it runs on shared references, taboo boundaries, and norms about *when* joking is appropriate:

- **Self-deprecating humor** — charming in the UK and US — reads as genuine incompetence in Japan, Germany, and China, where a supplier publicly running itself down triggers real doubts.
- **Irony and sarcasm** survive translation poorly almost everywhere; what lands as wit in English lands as *false statement* in markets with low irony tolerance (and subtitled sarcasm loses the tone markers that signal it).
- **Puns and wordplay** are untranslatable by definition — a joke about "pressing issues" in a hydraulic-press video simply doesn't exist in German. The adaptation choice is a *different* joke that plays the same role, or dropping the beat.
- **Norms about joking in business** vary outright: a light quip in a US corporate video is expected; the same beat in a Japanese B2B video can read as not taking the buyer seriously.

The rule of thumb: humor that's load-bearing — jokes the persuasion depends on — gets adapted per market by native copywriters, not translated. Humor that's decorative gets cut when it doesn't survive. Neither decision is a translator's to make alone; it belongs in the script-adaptation review with market-native reviewers, the same loop used for [scenario training content](/news/scenario-training-cultural-localization/).

## Symbols, colors, and calendar reality

Beyond body language, the frame is full of quiet signals:

- **Colors**: white is purity in the West and mourning in parts of Asia; red is luck in China and danger/debt elsewhere; green carries Islamic associations that are positive in the Gulf and meaningless in Brazil. The [color-localization rules](/news/image-icon-color-localization/) that apply to icons apply double to full-motion footage.
- **Calendars and holidays**: "summer shutdown" is August in Europe, meaningless in Australia (their summer is Christmas) and alien in the Gulf, where the rhythm is Ramadan and a Friday-Saturday weekend. Any video referencing seasons, holidays, or work rhythms needs per-market sense-checking.
- **People and settings**: who plays the engineer and who plays the customer, how people are dressed, what the office looks like — audiences read all of it as "is this company for people like us." An all-Western cast in a video for Southeast Asia isn't offensive; it's just quietly *not local*, which undercuts the message that you're committed to the market.

## The cultural audit: how to actually catch this

Ad hoc doesn't work; make it a stage in the [video translation workflow](/news/video-translation-workflow-steps/):

1. **Flag at script stage.** The translator/adaptor marks culturally loaded elements — humor, idioms, references — as they translate. Zero cost, catches the layer-three issues before production.
2. **Visual pass by market-native reviewers.** Someone from each target culture *watches the cut* (not the script) with a checklist: gestures, symbols, colors, people, settings, calendar references. One viewing per market, roughly an hour per video.
3. **Triage the findings.** Cut (cheap), replace in edit (moderate), re-shoot (rare — usually only hero footage), or accept-with-awareness (sometimes a mild mismatch is fine; decide consciously).
4. **Record decisions in the project glossary** so the next video inherits the findings — gestures and symbols are usually shared across a company's whole video library.

For [on-screen text and graphics](/news/on-screen-text-video-localization/), the audit runs alongside the localization pass you'd do anyway — the cultural reviewer just needs the checklist expanded.
