An exporter plans their third and fourth website languages. The shortlist writes itself: German, Japanese — the markets everyone localizes for. Then their distributor in Ho Chi Minh City mentions that two competitors launched Vietnamese sites last year and are picking up inquiries the English-only players never see. A quick check: Vietnam — 100 million people, manufacturing FDI darling, English proficiency ranking "low" on the EF index. Thailand — 70 million, "very low" English proficiency. Indonesia — 280 million, the world's fourth-largest population, "low" proficiency. Combined: 450 million people whose industrial buyers research suppliers in Vietnamese, Thai, and Bahasa Indonesia — languages almost no exporter website speaks. The safe-list languages are safe precisely because everyone serves them; the emerging languages are where a mid-size exporter can be the *only* supplier in their niche with a local-language presence. The question isn't whether these markets matter — it's when the ROI beats adding another European language.

## The demand-side case

Three structural facts drive the ROI:

1. **English doesn't reach the buyer.** EF English Proficiency data places Vietnam, Thailand, and Indonesia in the low-to-very-low bands. The procurement manager at a Thai auto-parts factory or an Indonesian food-processing plant reads English slowly and Thai/Indonesian natively. As in [India](/news/india-multilingual-content-strategy/) and the [mobile-first markets](/news/mobile-first-b2b-emerging-markets/), the shortlist gets built in the local language; English-only suppliers are invisible at the moment that matters.
2. **The competition vacuum is real.** Search your product's keywords in Vietnamese or Thai: the first page is local distributors, marketplaces, and at most one multinational. The exporter who publishes a credible local-language site doesn't fight for position 8 — they often define the category. The [long-tail keyword math](/news/industrial-long-tail-keywords/) that takes 18 months in German can work in 6 in Vietnamese.
3. **These are manufacturing economies, not just consumer markets.** Vietnam's electronics and machinery sectors, Thailand's automotive and food processing, Indonesia's resources and infrastructure build-out — the demand is industrial, exactly where exporter catalogs sell. And regional supply-chain shifts (China+1 strategies) are actively moving sourcing *into* these markets, creating buyers who are forming supplier relationships right now.

## The cost-side reality

Emerging-language localization is **cheaper to build and cheaper to maintain** than the big European languages:

- Translation rates for Vietnamese, Thai, and Indonesian typically run below German/Japanese rates.
- The site architecture is already paid for — adding language #4 or #5 to an existing [multilingual site](/news/how-many-languages-export-website/) is incremental: translation + review + deployment, not a rebuild.
- The maintenance burden (the real long-term cost, per the [sync discipline](/news/multilingual-content-sync-maintenance/)) scales with update frequency, not language prestige; a Vietnamese version of a stable catalog costs little to keep current.

The honest costs sit elsewhere: **finding quality reviewers** (you likely have no Vietnamese-speaking staff — your distributor becomes essential, as in the [in-context review model](/news/in-context-translation-review/)), **script and font handling** (Vietnamese Latin script with dense diacritics, Thai's no-space script with its own line-breaking rules — test rendering everywhere, per [multilingual DTP practice](/news/text-expansion-translation-layout/)), and **channel coverage** (these markets run on Zalo/LINE/WhatsApp respectively — your [contact architecture](/news/export-website-contact-channels/) should meet them there).

## The evaluation framework: when to place the bet

Score each candidate language on four axes, with your own numbers:

| Axis | What to check | Where the data lives |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing demand** | Inquiries, distributor activity, and traffic from that country in the last 12 months | Your CRM, inbox, and [GA4 geo reports](/news/ga4-export-website-metrics/) |
| **Search demand** | Local-language keyword volume for your product terms | [Multilingual keyword research](/news/multilingual-keyword-research-guide/) per language |
| **Competitive vacuum** | How many credible local-language supplier sites exist in your niche | Search your top 10 keywords in the local language; count real competitors |
| **Channel readiness** | Do you have a distributor/rep who can review content and handle local-language inquiries? | Your partner network — no channel, no point |

The decision rule: an emerging language beats a prestige language when existing country demand is real *and* the competitive vacuum is visible *and* someone can answer the inquiries it generates. All three conditions are testable in a week, cheaply.

## The staged entry (low-regret version)

1. **Stage 1 — landing presence** (~10 pages): homepage, top 3–5 products, company/profile, contact — enough to rank and convert, small enough to be excellent. Native review by the distributor's team.
2. **Stage 2 — measure for 2 quarters**: local-language organic traffic, inquiry volume and quality from the market, distributor feedback on usability of materials.
3. **Stage 3 — expand or hold**: full catalog + content marketing if the funnel proves out; keep the landing tier maintained if it's a slow burn; the architecture supports either.

One more consideration: **these languages compound regionally.** A Vietnamese site supports the Vietnamese-speaking diaspora business networks; Bahasa Indonesia gives you a head start on Malay (Malaysia) with modest adaptation; Thai unlocks a market notoriously loyal to suppliers who bothered. First-mover advantage in a language is durable — rankings, distributor mindshare, and the reputation of "the supplier who speaks our language" persist for years.

German and Japanese remain good languages.
