An industrial supplier ranks well in Mexico for "maquinaria CNC," then wonders why the same pages are invisible in Spain — where buyers search "mecanizado CNC" and "centro de mecanizado." The reverse happens too: a site optimized for Spain pulls impressions from Buenos Aires it can't convert and misses the procurement vocabulary of Madrid. Spanish is one language, but Spanish-language search is at least two markets with different vocabularies, different competitors, and different buyer intent.

Here is how to think about Spanish SEO when you sell to both sides of the Atlantic.

## The vocabulary split is commercial, not academic

Everyone knows "coche/carro/auto" and "ordenador/computadora." Those don't matter much — few B2B searches hinge on them. What matters is the industrial and commercial vocabulary:

| Concept | Spain | Latin America |
|---|---|---|
| Machining | mecanizado | maquinado (parts of LatAm) / mecanizado |
| Supplier | proveedor | proveedor / abastecedor (context) |
| Quote / RFQ | presupuesto | cotización |
| Invoice | factura (proforma) | factura / cotización previa |
| Shipping | envío / transporte | flete (much of LatAm) |
| Machinery | maquinaria | maquinaria / equipo |

The RFQ row alone should change your CTA copy: a "Solicitar presupuesto" button reads naturally in Spain and slightly off in Mexico, where "Solicitar cotización" is the standard. These are the terms buyers type, and the pattern from our [multilingual keyword research guide](/news/multilingual-keyword-research-guide/) applies with full force: research per market, never translate.

## Intent and competition differ by market

**Search volume concentrates differently.** Mexico, Colombia, Argentina, and Chile together dwarf Spain's search volume in most industrial categories — but the queries skew toward distributors and local stock ("distribuidor," "venta en México"), while Spain's skew toward manufacturers and EU compliance ("fabricante," "certificado CE"). The same product needs different supporting content per market.

**The competitive set is local.** In Spain you compete with Iberian and EU suppliers; in Mexico with Mexican and US suppliers; in Argentina with local players and Brazilian imports. Difficulty scores from a single "Spanish" database average these into uselessness — always pull volume and difficulty per country.

**English contamination varies.** US-influenced Mexican industry searches English technical terms heavily ("CNC machining services" typed from Monterrey is common). Spain searches more consistently in Spanish. Your Mexican pages may legitimately target English-adjacent terms that your Spain pages shouldn't.

## Site architecture: one locale or two?

Three options, in increasing effort:

**Option A: One neutral-Spanish section.** `/es/` with pan-regional vocabulary, `hreflang="es"` (no country). Works when LatAm and Spain are both secondary markets. You will rank imperfectly everywhere and convert slightly worse everywhere — acceptable at low stakes.

**Option B: One Spanish section + country-targeted landing pages.** `/es/` plus a handful of market pages ("Maquinaria CNC en México," "…en España") with market-specific vocabulary, currency, and contact routing. The pragmatic middle: captures country-modified searches ("proveedor X México") without duplicating the whole site.

**Option C: Two full locales.** `/es-mx/` and `/es-es/` (or `/es/` + `/es-la/`), with `hreflang` pairs. Correct when both markets are strategic. Do this only with real per-market copy — duplicating pages with find-and-replace vocabulary is exactly the thin-duplicate pattern the [hreflang mistakes article](/news/hreflang-mistakes-multilingual-b2b/) warns about.

Whichever you choose: hreflang entries need the language-country pairs (`es-mx`, `es-es`), an `x-default`, and reciprocal tags. Getting this wrong doesn't just waste the setup — Google can ignore the whole cluster.

## Link building and local signals

Backlinks carry country weight. A link from a Spanish industrial directory (soloStocks-style platforms, local chamber sites) helps Spain rankings more than pan-Spanish ones; Mexican industry directories and local B2B marketplaces help Mexico. Other local signals that move rankings per market:

- **Google Business Profile** for any local office or distributor — map-pack presence in-country
- **Local currency and phone numbers** on market pages (both users and local ranking signals)
- **Country-modified content**: case studies naming local customers or industries, shipping-to-country pages
- **Domain strategy**: a `.com.mx` outranks a subdirectory in Mexico when content is otherwise equal — but splits your domain authority. Subdirectories with good local signals are the right default; ccTLDs are for committed market entry.

## A realistic starting sequence

1. Run keyword research for Mexico and Spain separately (the two highest-volume markets for most exporters) — one week with native researchers.
2. Decide architecture from the revenue plan: A, B, or C above. Most B2B exporters should start at B.
3. Build or adapt the core pages with market-correct vocabulary, CTAs ("cotización" vs "presupuesto"), and contact routing.
4. Set up hreflang correctly and validate in Search Console per country — impressions and queries segmented by country will tell you within a quarter whether the split is working.

Spanish SEO done as one market is a compromise both markets can feel; done as two, it is one of the highest-leverage international SEO plays available — one language's worth of content serving 500 million speakers.
