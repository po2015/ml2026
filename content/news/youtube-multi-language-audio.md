Your product demo has 200,000 views in English. Your distributor in Monterrey shares it with a customer, who watches thirty seconds and bounces — not because the product is wrong, but because the narration is. Until recently the fix was ugly: separate channels per language, splitting your views and subscribers across a dozen URLs. YouTube's multi-language audio (MLA) feature changes the economics: one video, many audio tracks, one pile of watch time. But the companies getting results from it treat audio as one layer of a four-layer job.

## Layer 1: The audio track itself

MLA lets you upload additional audio tracks to an existing video; viewers get the track matching their interface language, or pick manually. What separates a professional track from a checkbox exercise:

- **Dub properly, not lazily.** An AI voice reading a raw machine translation over your CEO's footage is detectable within seconds and damages the brand it was meant to extend. The [dubbing workflow](/news/ai-dubbing-corporate-training-cost-quality/) that works for training video applies here: translated script adapted for lip-sync and timing, a voice matched to the speaker's register, and a native-speaker QC pass.
- **Keep the original bed.** Music and sound effects live on the M&E (music and effects) stem. If your original edit mixed everything into one track, you'll need to rebuild the bed — ask your video editor for stems *before* the next project, not after.
- **Match the market's expectation.** Some markets default to subtitles and find dubbing of business content odd; others strongly prefer audio. Our [subtitles-vs-dubbing breakdown](/news/subtitles-vs-dubbing-by-market/) maps this by region — check it before commissioning ten tracks.

## Layer 2: Metadata — where most efforts die

Here is the uncomfortable truth: a perfect Spanish audio track on a video titled in English will be found by almost no one searching in Spanish. YouTube search indexes title and description far more than audio content. MLA supports **translated titles and descriptions per language**, and this is where the ROI actually lives.

- **Translate titles for search intent, not fidelity.** "How to Choose a Ball Valve" might map to a differently-phrased query in German or Portuguese. Do real [keyword research per language](/news/multilingual-keyword-research-guide/) — a translated title nobody searches is metadata theater.
- **Localize the description's first 150 characters.** That's what shows in search snippets. Front-load the local keyword and the value proposition; put the localized landing-page link in the first lines, not the footer.
- **Tags and hashtags matter less, but localize them anyway** — they cost minutes and occasionally catch variant spellings.

## Layer 3: Subtitles still matter

Multi-language audio doesn't remove the need for subtitles; it changes their job:

- **Original-language captions** feed YouTube's understanding of the video and serve deaf/hard-of-hearing viewers — follow [SDH practice](/news/sdh-accessibility-subtitles/), not raw auto-captions.
- **Translated subtitles** cover viewers who prefer reading, watch muted (the majority of mobile viewers), or speak a language you didn't dub. Follow the [formatting rules](/news/subtitle-formatting-rules/) per language — line length and reading speed differ.
- **On-screen text** inside the video (charts, callouts, UI captures) stays untranslated by MLA. If your demo is text-heavy, consider per-market rendered versions for your top markets, as discussed in [on-screen text localization](/news/on-screen-text-video-localization/).

## Layer 4: Thumbnails and end screens

A thumbnail with English text undercuts a Spanish audio track before the play button is pressed. Options, in order of effort:

1. **Design text-light thumbnails** from the start — a product image, a face, one number. Localizes for free.
2. **Per-language thumbnail variants** for flagship videos (YouTube is rolling out thumbnail localization with MLA; where unavailable, choose imagery that carries no text).
3. **End screens and cards** point viewers somewhere — make sure that somewhere has a localized landing page, or the journey ends in an English dead end.

## The operating model

| Task | Once per video | Per language |
|---|---|---|
| Script translation + adaptation | — | ✓ |
| Voice recording / AI dub + QC | — | ✓ |
| M&E stem + mix | ✓ (if stems exist) | mix only |
| Title/description keyword research | — | ✓ |
| Subtitle files (source + translated) | ✓ source | ✓ |
| Thumbnail decision | ✓ | only if text-heavy |

The practical sequence: dub and localize metadata for your **top 10 videos in your top 2–3 markets first**. Measure per-language watch time and click-through for a quarter, then expand. A/B beats blanket coverage — the same logic as [choosing how many languages your website needs](/news/how-many-languages-export-website/).
