A buyer receives a quotation from a Chinese machinery supplier and does what buyers do before replying: searches the company name. The search results show the website — good — but the right-side knowledge panel is empty, and Maps shows nothing. For a moment the buyer wonders if the company is real. That moment of doubt is what Google Business Profile (GBP) exists to prevent, and it costs nothing but an afternoon. Exporters often skip GBP entirely ("we don't serve local customers") or set it up carelessly (a half-empty profile that looks worse than none). Both misunderstand what the profile is for: not walk-in traffic, but **verification**. When someone searches your brand — and every serious buyer does before signing — the profile confirms you exist, where you exist, and that you're an operating business with photos, products, and reviews. Here's how to set it up correctly for an export business.

## Why GBP matters even when you don't sell locally

- **The brand-search panel.** Search your company name: GBP populates the knowledge panel with address, hours, photos, reviews, and links. An empty panel isn't neutral — it reads as "small or not established," the opposite of the [trust signals](/news/b2b-website-trust-signals/) your website works to project.
- **Maps as a credibility check.** Procurement staff routinely verify supplier addresses on satellite view (is that really a factory?). A claimed, accurate listing with real photos passes that check; an absent or wrong one starts an awkward email thread.
- **It feeds Google's entity understanding.** GBP data reinforces your [Organization schema](/news/schema-markup-manufacturer-websites/) and knowledge-graph presence — the machine-readable identity that search and AI systems resolve you by.
- **Reviews accumulate somewhere anyway.** Better on a profile you manage, where you can respond, than on third-party sites you don't watch.

## Setup, the right order

1. **Claim and verify** at business.google.com with a company-controlled Google account (not an employee's personal Gmail — the #1 ownership disaster). Verification methods vary (postcard, phone, video, or instant for Search Console-verified domains); budget 1–2 weeks if it needs a video or postcard.
2. **Core data, exactly consistent**: legal/official business name (no keyword stuffing — "ABC Machinery Co., Ltd.", not "ABC Machinery Best CNC Valve Manufacturer Exporter"; stuffing is the top suspension trigger), address, phone, website URL. This NAP data must match your website footer, [directory listings](/news/b2b-backlink-building-exporters/), and trade-fair registrations character-for-character — inconsistency erodes the local-SEO value and confuses the entity matching.
3. **Category selection**: pick the primary category closest to what you *make* ("Machinery manufacturer," "Industrial equipment supplier"), plus 2–3 relevant secondary categories. Categories drive which searches show your profile.
4. **The trust content**: 10+ real photos (factory exterior with signage, production floor, team, products, certifications), business hours, a 750-character description written for buyers (what you make, whom you serve, certifications), and — often missed — **products**: GBP's product section takes your catalog highlights with images and links, free shelf space in the brand panel.
5. **Attributes and opening date**: "Identifies as..." attributes as applicable; founding date feeds the knowledge panel's credibility line.

## The exporter-specific questions

**"We have a factory in China and an office in Germany — one profile or two?"** Two profiles, one per physical location, each with its local address, phone, and — importantly — the language and content of its market. The German office profile serves European buyers' verification; the factory profile serves "is this real" checks. Never create profiles for locations that aren't genuine staffed offices — virtual addresses and mailbox "offices" violate guidelines and get profiles (sometimes all of them) suspended.

**"We exhibit abroad but have no office there."** Then no profile there. GBP is for physical locations; your international reach belongs to the website, [multilingual SEO](/news/international-seo-guide-chinese-manufacturers/), and the description text — not to fake Map pins.

**"Do service-area settings apply to us?"** Barely — that feature is for plumbers and cleaners who visit customers. Manufacturers list their address and ship worldwide; say "worldwide" in the description, not in service-area fields.

## The pitfalls that suspend or sink profiles

- **Keyword-stuffed business names** — the most common suspension cause, and competitors can report it (they do).
- **Virtual offices and P.O. boxes** as locations — against guidelines, increasingly detected by video verification.
- **Review solicitation gone wrong**: incentivized reviews violate policy; a sudden burst of 5-star reviews from accounts with no history looks exactly like what it is. The legitimate path: ask *real* customers after successful deliveries, consistently, a few per month — and respond to every review, including (especially) the bad ones, professionally and in the reviewer's language.
- **Abandoned profiles**: outdated hours, old address after a move, unanswered Q&A (anyone can ask — and anyone can *answer*, including trolls). GBP belongs on the [website maintenance checklist](/news/website-maintenance-plan-exporters/): quarterly review, post updates occasionally (GBP posts — product news, fair appearances — keep the profile visibly alive).
- **Ownership sprawl**: the ex-employee's Gmail that owns the profile. Audit it now, while you still can.

## The 30-minute quarterly routine

Verify NAP accuracy (post-move especially), add 2–3 fresh photos, publish one post (product launch, fair presence, certification), respond to new reviews and Q&A, check the Insights tab for how buyers find you. That's it — the smallest ongoing effort in local marketing, protecting the first thing a buyer sees after your own website.
