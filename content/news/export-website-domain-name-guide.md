Exporters spend weeks on website design and ten minutes on the domain name — usually registering whatever the founder typed first. The domain then sits in every email address, quotation, business card, and Google result for years. It is the cheapest part of the website and one of the hardest to change later, so it deserves a more deliberate decision than it usually gets.

This guide covers what actually matters when choosing a domain for an export-focused B2B site: the extension question, the naming rules that survive international buyers, and the SEO realities that are smaller than most people think.

## The extension question: .com vs ccTLD vs everything else

| Extension | Buyer perception | SEO behavior | When to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| `.com` | Global default; neutral, professional | No geographic bias; works everywhere | Default choice for an export site |
| ccTLD (`.de`, `.fr`, `.co.jp`) | Strong local trust in that market | Strong local ranking signal, weak elsewhere | Dedicated site for one major market |
| `.cn` | Signals "China domestic" to foreign buyers | Ranks in China, needs ICP license for local hosting | Your Chinese domestic site, not export |
| New gTLDs (`.io`, `.tech`, `.store`) | Mixed; some buyers read them as startups or spam | Treated like generic TLDs, no boost | Only if the .com is truly unavailable |
| `.net`, `.org` | Dated but harmless | Neutral | Acceptable fallback |

The rule of thumb: **one global `.com` as your main export site**, plus a ccTLD only when a single market is big enough to justify a dedicated, fully localized site (Germany and Japan are the classic cases). Registering ten ccTLDs and redirecting them all to the `.com` is fine for brand protection — it just does nothing for rankings.

<div class="notice notice-warning">
<strong>Warning</strong>
If your company name's .com is taken by an active business in a related industry, do not register a confusingly similar variant (hyphens, added "-global", plural). Buyers who mistype will land on a competitor or a parked page, and email will leak to the wrong domain.
</div>

## Naming rules that survive international buyers

A good export domain passes five tests:

1. **Pronounceable on the phone.** A buyer in São Paulo should be able to say your domain to a colleague without spelling it letter by letter. Avoid letter sequences that are natural in Pinyin but opaque in English (`xinjiahong`, `zhonglixin`) — foreign buyers cannot remember, spell, or type them.
2. **Short enough to type.** Under 15 characters is the practical ceiling. Every extra character adds typo rates on mobile.
3. **No hyphens or numbers.** Hyphens get forgotten ("was it abc-machinery or abcmachinery?") and read as low-budget. Numbers create ambiguity (is "4u" the digit or the word?).
4. **No unintended meanings.** Check the name against English, Spanish, and your target markets' slang. Brand names that are innocent in Chinese have embarrassed companies abroad; fifteen minutes of checking avoids this.
5. **Matches your company identity.** The domain should connect to the name on your business license, Alibaba store, or trade show booth. A buyer who met "Dongguan Precision Parts Co." at Hannover Messe will search that name — if your domain is `bestcncparts4u.com`, they will not find you.

Keywords in the domain (`precision-cnc-parts.com`) carry minimal ranking weight today. If a clean keyword domain is available and reads naturally, take it; do not sacrifice the brand name for it.

## What actually matters for SEO (and what doesn't)

Let us separate signal from folklore:

**Does not matter (or barely):**
- Keywords in the domain — Google has discounted exact-match domains for years; brand strength outweighs them.
- Domain age of a *new* registration — an aged domain helps only if it has a clean, relevant history.
- Registering for 10 years in advance — no ranking effect, though it is cheap insurance against accidental expiry.

**Actually matters:**
- **Clean history.** A previously used domain may carry spam backlinks or a manual penalty. Check any candidate in the Wayback Machine and a backlink tool before buying; a "cheap premium domain" with a spammy past can cost you months.
- **HTTPS and technical setup**, which have nothing to do with the name but everything to do with whether the site ranks at all.
- **Consistency.** All language versions should live on one domain (subdirectories like `/de/`, `/es/`) so every market's content builds the same domain authority. Splitting languages across separate domains splits your ranking power. See our [comparison of international search engines](/news/international-search-engines-comparison/) for where this matters most.
- **Email on the same domain.** Quotes sent from `@163.com` or `@qq.com` addresses get filtered and distrusted by Western corporate mail servers. The domain decision is also your email deliverability decision.

## The registration checklist

Before you pay:

1. **Check history**: Wayback Machine snapshots, backlink profile, blacklist status.
2. **Check trademarks**: search your target markets' trademark databases for conflicts.
3. **Register defensively**: the `.com` plus obvious misspellings and the ccTLDs of your top two markets; redirect them all to the main site.
4. **Use a registrar you control directly** — not your web agency's account. Domains held hostage by a former vendor are a depressingly common reason exporters lose their site.
5. **Turn on auto-renew and registry lock**, and use a dedicated, monitored email address for the registrar account.

## The bottom line

Choose a short, pronounceable `.com` that matches your company identity, verify its history, register it in your own name, and put every language version on it. That decision, made once and correctly, quietly supports every inquiry your website generates for the next decade.
