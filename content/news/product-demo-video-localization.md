Every exporter has one: the product demo video that closes deals. The sales team sends it before every call, the distributor asks for it monthly, and it converts — in English. Then a buyer in São Paulo or Jeddah gets the link and watches a machine they can't understand narrated in a language they only half follow. Re-shooting per market is economically absurd for most B2B products. The good news: a well-planned demo localizes in layers, and most layers cost far less than a shoot day. Here's the layer-by-layer approach.

## Layer 0: Know what's actually in your video

Before any budget discussion, inventory the video's components, because each localizes differently:

| Component | Examples | Localization options |
|---|---|---|
| Spoken narration | Voiceover, presenter to camera | Dub / voiceover / subtitles |
| On-screen text | Titles, callouts, spec overlays | Edit text layers / overlay patches |
| Screen or UI captures | Software demos, HMI panels | Re-capture in target language / annotate |
| Physical footage | Machine running, hands working | Usually nothing — the universal layer |
| Music & effects | Bed, whooshes, clicks | Nothing (keep the M&E stem) |
| End cards & CTAs | Contact info, URLs | Rebuild per market |

Two assets determine whether this is cheap or painful: **the project file** (Premiere/After Effects/DaVinci — with text on separate layers) and **the M&E stem** (music/effects without voice). If you have both, layers 1–3 are days of work. If you have only a flattened MP4, every text change becomes pixel surgery. Ask your video producer for both *today*, even if localization is a year away.

## Layer 1: Narration — dub or subtitle?

The market decides, not you. Our [subtitles-vs-dubbing map](/news/subtitles-vs-dubbing-by-market/) breaks down preferences by region; the short version: Latin America, France, Germany, and the Arab world lean dubbing; Nordics, Netherlands, and much of Asia-Pacific accept subtitles happily even for marketing content.

For B2B demos specifically, **voiceover-style dubbing** (narration replaced, no lip-sync needed since most demos are voice-over-footage rather than talking heads) hits the sweet spot: affordable with AI voices plus native QC, fast, and quality is controlled by the script adaptation — the discipline from our [script adaptation guide](/news/dubbing-script-adaptation-timing/) applies line by line. Budget roughly per the [cost framework](/news/ai-dubbing-corporate-training-cost-quality/): AI narration with human review runs a fraction of studio talent, and for a 4-minute demo the absolute numbers are small either way.

## Layer 2: On-screen text

Spec callouts ("IP67", "45 Nm", "CE") are often universal — numbers and standards don't translate. What does: descriptive labels, benefit headlines, and section titles. From the project file, swapping text layers is mechanical — but watch the two classic traps:

- **Text expansion**: German and Russian run 20–30% longer; the animated callout that elegantly fit "Fast setup" must now fit "Schnelle Einrichtung". Reduce font size cautiously, abbreviate, or re-time the animation — the same [expansion discipline](/news/text-expansion-translation-layout/) as documents.
- **Animation timing**: text that wipes on letter-by-letter or in sync with narration must be re-timed to the new audio, since sentence structure differs per language.

No project file? Overlaid "patch" graphics (a clean plate matching the background with new text) cover most cases; heavily animated sequences may justify recreating just that segment.

## Layer 3: UI and screen captures

If the demo shows your product's software interface or HMI panel, the decision tree is short:

- **Product ships with a localized UI** → re-capture the screen recordings in the target-language UI. Nothing else looks credible.
- **Product UI is English-only** → keep the English capture and localize *around* it: translated narration explains what's shown, and a brief translated callout can label key UI elements. Never fake a localized UI that doesn't exist — buyers who purchase will discover the truth.

## Layer 4: The wrapper — thumbnails, end cards, metadata

The end card carries your CTA: rebuild it per market with the local contact path (distributor name, local number, localized landing page URL). And if the demo lives on YouTube, the metadata is half the battle — localized titles, descriptions, and multi-language audio tracks, per our [YouTube localization guide](/news/youtube-multi-language-audio/). A beautifully localized video with English-only metadata is findable only by people who didn't need it localized.

## The pragmatic rollout

Don't localize everything for everyone. Sequence it:

1. **Top demo × top 2–3 markets** first (full treatment: dubbed narration, localized text, market end card).
2. **Subtitles only** for the long tail of videos and markets — cheap, fast, and honest.
3. **Measure**: per-market watch time and inquiry attribution decide where the next full localization goes — the same [corporate video playbook](/news/video-localization-corporate-training-guide/) that works for training content.

One more upstream note: the next time you *produce* a demo, shoot it for localization — text on layers, stems delivered, minimal on-camera speech, universal visuals. That one brief to your videographer cuts future localization cost by half or more.
