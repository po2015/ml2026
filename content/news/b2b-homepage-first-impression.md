A buyer lands on your homepage from Google. They do not read it — they interrogate it, in a fixed order, for about ten seconds. If the answers come easily, you get another two minutes. If not, the tab closes and the next supplier on the results page gets the inquiry. This is not a theory about attention spans; it is how procurement professionals triage dozens of potential suppliers per sourcing project.

Here is what gets judged, in the order it gets judged, and what to fix first.

## Second 0–2: "What do you make, and is it for me?"

The headline answers this or nothing does. "Precision CNC machining for medical and aerospace OEMs" passes. "Welcome to XXX Technology Co., Ltd." fails. "Innovative solutions for a better tomorrow" fails harder — it is not even wrong, just empty. The buyer is matching your headline against the part in their hand; make the match easy.

Directly under the headline, the hero image gets the same treatment. Factory floor, machines, parts, applications — credible. Generic stock handshake, city skyline, or a gradient with abstract shapes — the buyer learns nothing and starts doubting there is anything to learn. We covered the copy side of this in [English copywriting for manufacturers](/news/english-copywriting-manufacturer-websites/); the principle is identical: specifics persuade.

## Second 2–5: "Are you real, and are you big enough?"

Now the buyer scans for verifiable anchors, in roughly this order:

1. **Certifications**: ISO 9001, IATF 16949, ISO 13485 — with numbers or logos that look claimed, not clip-art
2. **Numbers**: founded year, factory area, machine count, export share
3. **Names**: recognizable customers, industries, or export markets ("supplying Tier-1 automotive since 2013")
4. **Geography**: where the factory actually is — hiding this triggers the trading-company suspicion

A buyer who cannot confirm at least two of these in five seconds mentally files you as "verify later" — and later rarely comes. This is why a certifications strip and a three-number fact bar ("Est. 2009 · 12,000 m² · 45 CNC centers") outperform any slogan ever written.

## Second 5–10: "Can I reach a human, and will they answer?"

The last check before committing more time: the contact path. Buyers look for a visible email, a phone number with a country code, a response-time commitment, and increasingly WhatsApp. A contact form as the *only* option reads as a black hole — especially to senior buyers who email from their own client with attachments. Put a real email address in the header or footer. The spam it attracts is a rounding error next to the inquiries it saves.

## The silent disqualifiers

These don't lose the ten-second test; they lose it before it starts:

- **Load time over ~3 seconds** on a hotel/airport connection. Heavy hero videos and unoptimized images are the usual cause. Buyers sourcing from trade fair Wi-Fi will not wait.
- **Broken English in the first screen.** One grammar error in the headline area outweighs ten perfect paragraphs below — it announces the site wasn't built by people who serve export markets daily.
- **The copyright year says 2021**, or the news section's latest post is two years old. Buyers read this as "possibly out of business."
- **Mixed languages on the English version** — a Chinese character in a caption, an untranslated PDF filename. Small leak, large doubt.

## The 10-second audit

Run this quarterly, on a phone, on mobile data:

| Check | Pass standard |
|---|---|
| Headline states product + customer | Readable without scrolling, under 10 words |
| Hero image | Shows your actual product/factory/application |
| Proof bar | 2+ verifiable facts visible on first screen |
| Contact path | Email + phone visible without scrolling |
| Load time | First screen under 3s on 4G |
| Freshness | Copyright year current; some 2026 content visible |

Failing one line is normal. Failing three means the homepage is filtering out buyers you never knew visited.

The encouraging part: none of these fixes require a redesign. A headline rewrite, a fact bar, a real photo, and a visible email address are a one-week project — and they compound, because every future visitor gets the better version.
