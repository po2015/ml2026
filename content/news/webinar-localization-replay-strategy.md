Your product team just ran the best webinar of the year: 400 registrants, a sharp demo, forty minutes of Q&A gold. The recording goes into a shared drive, marketing posts it on the English resource page, and everyone moves on. Six weeks later your DACH sales lead asks why there's no German version for a prospect who's "very interested but doesn't do calls in English." The replay was the highest-value content asset you produced all quarter — and in four of your five markets, it effectively doesn't exist. This is the fixable part: one webinar recording can become a full set of localized replay assets in about two weeks per language, if you treat localization as part of the production plan instead of an afterthought.

## Start with the transcript, not the video

Every localization decision downstream gets cheaper and more accurate when the transcript comes first. Export the platform's auto-transcript the day the webinar ends (Zoom, Teams, and ON24 all generate one), then spend an hour cleaning it: fix product names, model numbers, and the speaker's mangled competitor mentions, and mark sections that shouldn't be localized at all — internal roadmap comments, a joke that only works in English, the ten minutes of dead air while someone fixed screen sharing.

That cleaned transcript becomes the single source for everything else: translation, subtitle timing, dubbing scripts, the replay page copy, and the SEO text. Teams that skip this step end up paying for it three times — once when the subtitle vendor re-transcribes from audio, once when the translator works from untimed text and the timing no longer fits, and once when someone rewrites the landing page from memory. The full sequence maps closely to the standard [video translation workflow](/news/video-translation-workflow-steps/), with one webinar-specific addition: Q&A segments need a human pass no matter what, because auto-transcription accuracy drops sharply on unscripted, accented, cross-talk audio — the [ASR accuracy numbers](/news/ai-subtitling-accuracy-benchmarks/) bear this out.

## Subtitles, AI dubbing, or voiceover: the replay decision

This is the decision that sets your budget and timeline, so make it per market, not once globally. The three realistic options for a recorded webinar:

| | Subtitles | AI dubbing | Human voiceover |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost per language (60 min) | Low — translation + timing | Low–medium — TTS + script adaptation + QC | High — casting, studio, mix |
| Turnaround | 2–4 days | 3–5 days | 1–3 weeks |
| Viewer experience | Original voice preserved; reading load | Natural to listen to; occasional flatness on emotional beats | Broadcast-quality; strongest for brand content |
| Handles slides/screen-share well | Yes — viewer reads subs, watches screen | Yes | Yes |
| Handles Q&A and humor | Best — nuance stays in original audio | Weakest — TTS flattens banter | Good, but expensive for loose segments |
| Editing burden on slide text | None | None | None |
| Best fit | Technical demos, expert panels, Q&A-heavy replays | Marketing webinars, product launches, executive keynotes | Flagship brand content, high-stakes buyer journeys |

The blunt version: default to subtitles for anything technical or Q&A-heavy, use AI dubbing where the replay is a marketing asset that needs to feel produced, and reserve human voiceover for the one webinar per year that anchors a campaign. Market expectations matter too — German business audiences subtitle readily, while Spanish and Japanese viewers show stronger dubbing preference; the per-market breakdown in [subtitles vs. dubbing by market](/news/subtitles-vs-dubbing-by-market/) is worth reading before you standardize. A hybrid that works well for long webinars: AI dub the scripted presentation half, subtitle the Q&A half. If the replay will live on YouTube, [multi-language audio tracks](/news/youtube-multi-language-audio/) now let one video carry every dub without splitting your view count across five uploads.

## Slides and on-screen text are a separate workstream

Audio is only half the asset. A webinar replay is mostly slides with a talking head in the corner, and every one of those slides carries text: titles, bullet points, chart labels, the demo's UI, the legal disclaimer in the footer. Subtitled audio over English slides is the single most common half-localized webinar we see — the viewer hears their language and reads someone else's.

Decide early between two approaches:

1. **Localized slide rebuild.** Export the deck, translate it (watch for [text expansion](/news/subtitle-formatting-rules/) — German runs roughly 30% longer than English), and re-cut the video to swap slides. Highest quality, and required if the webinar was mostly screen-share of dense slides. Budget an editor day per language.
2. **On-screen text overlays.** Leave the video untouched and add translated text overlays or lower-thirds over key slides. Cheaper and faster, acceptable when slides are simple and the speaker carries the content.

Either way, audit the recording frame by frame for text you forgot: browser tabs during the demo, a chat window left visible, file names on a shared desktop. The same rules that apply to [on-screen text in video localization](/news/on-screen-text-video-localization/) apply here, with the added wrinkle that webinar screen-shares are full of text nobody staged.

## Chaptering and SEO: make the replay findable

A 55-minute unchaptered replay in five languages is five unwatchable blobs. Chaptering is where localization and SEO overlap, and it costs almost nothing:

- **Chapter per language, from the transcript.** Your cleaned transcript already has topic boundaries. Cut chapters there — "Introduction," "Live demo: configuration," "Q&A: pricing" — and translate chapter titles natively, not literally.
- **One landing page per market, not per video.** Each language gets its own replay page: localized title and meta description, a native-language summary, the chapter list, and the transcript (or an edited excerpt) as on-page text. That transcript is what lets the page rank for long-tail queries in that language — video alone gives search engines almost nothing.
- **Reuse the Q&A.** Ten localized Q&A answers from one webinar can seed that market's FAQ page, a follow-up blog post, or sales enablement snippets. The localization cost is already sunk; the content reuse is nearly free.
- **Mind the file formats.** Deliver subtitles as [SRT or VTT](/news/subtitle-file-formats-srt-vtt/) depending on the player, and keep the timed source files in your translation memory — the next webinar on the same product reuses 40% of the terminology.

Treated this way, the replay page behaves like the rest of your localized site and pulls its own organic traffic, instead of being a video embed that only existing leads ever see. The same repurposing logic drives [marketing video localization for ads](/news/marketing-video-localization-ads/) — one shoot, many market-specific cuts.

## Cost and timing: what to actually budget

For a typical 60-minute B2B webinar, realistic per-language numbers:

- **Subtitles only:** transcript cleanup, translation, timing, and QC in 2–4 working days. This is the baseline every market should get.
- **Add slide rebuild:** plus roughly one editor day and a review cycle.
- **AI dubbing:** plus 1–2 days for script adaptation, TTS generation, and a native-speaker QC pass — never ship AI dubbing without that pass; pronunciation errors on your own product name are how dubs die.
- **Human voiceover:** plus 1–3 weeks depending on studio scheduling.

The trade-off that actually matters is sequencing, not price. Subtitles for all five markets can ship inside a week of the webinar, while the content is still fresh and the registration list is still warm. Dubs take longer — so run them in parallel only for the two or three markets where the replay is a campaign asset, and let subtitles carry the rest. Publishing a subtitled replay this week beats publishing a dubbed one next month, because webinar content decays fast.
