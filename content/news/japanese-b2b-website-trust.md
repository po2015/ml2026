A German buyer scans your homepage for certifications and equipment lists; an American wants the value proposition in five seconds. A Japanese procurement manager does something different: they *read* — the company profile, the history page, the quality policy, the org chart-adjacent details that Western sites bury or omit. Japan is the world's third-largest B2B e-commerce market and one of the most demanding web audiences on earth. Companies that translate their existing site word-for-word usually fail there; companies that understand what Japanese buyers are actually looking for find a loyal, high-value market. The difference is three things: formality, detail, and a specific architecture of trust.

## Formality is a feature, not friction

Japanese business communication runs on register — keigo (honorific language) is not decorative politeness; it's the grammatical encoding of the business relationship. Consequences for your website:

- **Copy must be written in proper business Japanese**, desu/masu form at minimum, with appropriate honorifics for customer-facing pages. Casual, punchy, startup-toned Japanese — the kind a literal translation of English marketing copy produces — reads as either foreign or unserious. This is a transcreation job, not a translation job.
- **Indirectness is normal.** English B2B copy leads with the claim ("The fastest lead times in the industry"). Japanese B2B copy establishes context and reliability first; over-claiming triggers skepticism rather than interest. Tone down superlatives; replace them with specifics.
- **Errors are costlier.** A typo on your English site is sloppiness; a keigo error on your Japanese site signals that you haven't actually done business in Japan. Native review is non-negotiable — the same bar as [German-market expectations](/news/german-market-b2b-buyer-expectations/), applied to language register rather than engineering detail.

## Detail: the information appetite is real

Western conversion wisdom says cut copy, remove pages, simplify. Japanese B2B buyers reward the opposite instinct. They expect to find:

- **A complete company profile (会社概要)**: founding year, capital, employee count, executive names, bank relationships, history timeline. This page — an afterthought in the West — is a top-five page on Japanese B2B sites. Its absence reads as evasiveness.
- **Exhaustive specifications.** Where a US site offers a marketing datasheet, Japanese buyers expect the full spec table: every dimension, tolerance, material, and standard, downloadable as a proper PDF. The [trust-signal logic](/news/b2b-website-trust-signals/) is universal; Japan turns the dial to maximum.
- **Process documentation**: quality-control flowcharts, factory information, certification copies with numbers and expiry dates visible.
- **Detailed case studies** with customer industry, application, and measurable outcomes — anonymized is acceptable, vague is not.

This aligns with a deeper pattern: Japanese procurement is consensus-driven (ringi), and your website's reader must forward internally to people who will never visit it. Dense, self-contained, printable documentation travels through that process; a minimalist landing page dies in it.

## The trust architecture

Trust in Japanese B2B is built through specifics and intermediaries, in roughly this order:

1. **A Japanese legal presence or named partner.** A Tokyo/Osaka office address, a Japanese distributor with a name and phone number, or at minimum a Japanese-language contact path with a named human. "Contact our global team" fails here more than anywhere else we've measured.
2. **Japanese customer references.** Having supplied *other Japanese companies* is the single strongest signal — it proves you survived someone's audit already. Even one named or describable Japanese customer outweighs a dozen Western logos.
3. **Physical evidence**: factory photos, exhibition presence (Japanese buyers meet suppliers at trade shows and check the website afterward — the site must match what they saw), quality-system documentation.
4. **Formats and finish.** Dates as 2026年12月28日, yen pricing where relevant, proper Japanese typography with adequate line height — the [CJK font discipline](/news/cjk-latin-font-pairing/) applies fully. A Japanese page typeset in a fallback font with Western punctuation spacing announces its foreignness before a word is read.

## Practical scope decisions

- **Japanese is one language, one market** — no regional-variant problem like [Spanish](/news/latin-america-spanish-variants/). One well-built Japanese section serves the whole market.
- **SEO runs through Google** (dominant in Japan) with Yahoo! Japan still meaningful; keyword behavior differs from English — buyers search in Japanese for local sourcing but often in English for overseas suppliers. Run [native keyword research](/news/multilingual-keyword-research-guide/) before assuming which terms to target.
- **Forms: keep them short but formal.** Japanese users complete forms at lower rates than Western users; ask for company, name (one field), email, and message — and expect many buyers to prefer email or phone anyway. Publish both prominently.
- **Response expectations**: reply within one business day, in Japanese, from a named person. The first email exchange sets the relationship's trajectory.

## The payoff side of the ledger

Japan's demands are front-loaded: once a Japanese company qualifies you as a supplier, relationships are long, reorder rates are high, and price pressure is lower than in most markets. The website's job is to survive the initial scrutiny — to be detailed enough, formal enough, and verifiable enough that you make the shortlist the analyst presents internally.
