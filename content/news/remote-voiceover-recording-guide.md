A training manager commissions voiceover in six languages. Five years ago this meant six studio bookings in six cities; today every one of those voices records from a home studio — faster, cheaper, and, when specified properly, indistinguishable from facility recordings. But "recorded remotely" covers everything from a broadcast-grade treated booth with a $1,000 microphone to a laptop mic at a kitchen table, and the deliverables arrive with your company's name on them either way. The buyer's job isn't to audit acoustics — it's to *specify* quality so it can be checked. Here's the specification, the checks, and the honest limits.

## Why remote is the default now

Professional home studios crossed the quality threshold years ago: condenser microphones, treated spaces, and clean preamps are standard kit for working voice talent worldwide. For multilingual projects the economics are decisive — native talent recorded *in-country* (a real Berlin voice for German, a real São Paulo voice for PT-BR), no studio logistics per city, revisions turned around in days, and casting choice across the whole market rather than whoever lives near a studio. The [human-vs-AI voice decision](/news/ai-dubbing-vs-human-voice/) is unaffected: remote recording is how the *human* side of that choice gets delivered.

## The specification to send with every booking

Quality problems are almost always specification gaps. Five lines in the booking message prevent them:

| Spec | What to ask for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Format | WAV, 48 kHz, 24-bit, mono | Editing/mastering headroom; MP3 source files can't be un-compressed later |
| Noise floor | −60 dB RMS or quieter in silences | The kitchen-refrigerator test, made measurable |
| Room | Treated/dry recording, no audible reverb | Reverb can't be removed cleanly; a dry take can always be sweetened |
| Level | Peaks around −6 dBFS, no clipping | Consistent headroom across languages and talents |
| File naming & takes | Agreed convention; pronunciation-critical terms read twice | Painless assembly across six languages and dozens of files |

Two additions for multilingual series: a **pronunciation lexicon** (brand names, product names, technical terms — the [lexicon discipline](/news/ai-voice-pronunciation-lexicon/) applies to humans exactly as to AI voices), and **reference audio** — a sample of the tone you want (pace, energy, formality), because "professional and warm" translates into six languages six different ways without one.

## Checking what arrives

You don't need an audio engineering degree to QC remote files — a 10-minute pass per language catches everything that matters:

1. **The silence test.** Open the file, listen to the first 3 seconds of "silence" with headphones at raised volume. Hiss, hum, refrigerator, traffic — all audible immediately, all rejection reasons.
2. **The room test.** Clap your hands mentally: does the voice sound like it's in a box (bathroom reverb) or in fabric (dry, close)? Boxy = reshoot.
3. **The consistency test.** Play 15 seconds of the new language against 15 seconds of an existing one. Perceived loudness and tone should sit in the same family — a [series that jumps 6 dB between languages](/news/dubbing-audio-qa-checklist/) sounds assembled, not produced. (Minor level differences are a mastering fix; gross ones aren't.)
4. **The script fidelity test.** Native reviewer spot-checks three random passages against the script, plus every number, product name, and safety-critical line. Remote or not, this is where [voiceover style](/news/elearning-voiceover-narration-styles/) and accuracy get verified.
5. **The technical pass.** Sample rate/bit depth as booked, no clipped peaks, filenames per convention. Thirty seconds of file properties.

If the project is customer-facing or regulated, finish with the same [audio QA checklist](/news/dubbing-audio-qa-checklist/) you'd run on studio output — the channel changed, the bar didn't.

## Live-directed sessions: the upgrade worth knowing

Remote doesn't have to mean unsupervised. Standard practice for important scripts: the talent records while you (or your localization PM) listen in live — SessionLink, Source-Connect, or a plain video call with the raw audio recorded at the talent's end. Direction on pace, emphasis, and pronunciation happens in the session instead of across three revision rounds. For [timing-critical dubbing work](/news/dubbing-script-adaptation-timing/), live direction is close to mandatory; for straightforward narration, it's the difference between one round and three. Cost: typically a modest session fee on top of the recording rate.

## Where remote genuinely can't go

Honesty section. Remote recording struggles with: **ensembles** (two or more voices that must react to each other — dialogue timing suffers when recorded separately, though "remote ensemble" workflows with live link-ups mostly solve it); **whisper-quiet or very loud dynamic performances** (home booths have noise floors and neighbors); and **latency-sensitive sync work** like tight lip-sync, which wants an engineer riding levels in the room. For a six-language corporate narration series, none of this applies. For your brand film with an emotional two-hander dialogue, budget a facility session in the one or two markets that matter most and remote-record the rest.
