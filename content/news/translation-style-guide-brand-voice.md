A marketing director reviews the company's three newest language sites side by side and feels something she can't immediately name: they're all *correct*, but they're not the same company. The English original is punchy — short sentences, active voice, a bit of swagger. The German version is dense and formal. The Spanish is warm and expansive. The Japanese reads like it was written by a different, much more apologetic organization. Nothing was mistranslated; everything was *re-voiced*, because each translator made reasonable default choices in a vacuum. This is what happens without a translation style guide: your brand voice becomes whatever each translator's defaults happen to be. The fix is a document — typically 5–15 pages — that tells every linguist who touches your content *how your company sounds*, in their language.

## What a translation style guide actually contains

Not grammar rules (translators know grammar) — your company's *decisions*:

| Section | What it decides | Example entries |
|---|---|---|
| **Voice & tone** | The personality, with do/don't examples | "Confident but never arrogant; we say 'our valve outlasts,' never 'the best valve on the market'" |
| **Formality level** | Register per language | German: Sie-form, direct style; Japanese: です・ます, no excessive honorifics; Spanish: usted, warm but professional |
| **Sentence & rhythm rules** | How prose should feel | "Sentences under 25 words in DE/EN; active voice; no noun chains over 3 words" |
| **Terminology pointer** | Link to the [termbase](/news/terminology-management-termbase/) | Approved terms, forbidden variants, untranslatables (model numbers, product names) |
| **Mechanics** | The thousand small conventions | Number formats (1.000,5 vs 1,000.5), date formats, units (metric, with imperial in parentheses where relevant), currency, capitalization of headings |
| **Brand language** | Taglines, boilerplates, naming rules | Approved translations of the tagline; how product-family names behave in each language |
| **What NOT to translate** | Explicit keep-as-is list | Brand names, UI labels referenced in docs, certification marks |

The guide's test: a translator who has never worked for you should produce on-brand text using only this document plus the termbase. If they have to email you questions about tone, the guide has a gap.

## Why it pays (beyond "consistency")

1. **It converts quality from luck into process.** Without a guide, voice consistency depends on getting the same translator forever — fragile. With one, any qualified linguist lands near the target on the first pass, which is exactly what [scaling translation across vendors](/news/localization-vendor-brief-template/) requires.
2. **Review cycles shorten.** Reviewers stop rewriting tone (the most subjective, time-consuming feedback) and check against written rules instead. Disputes become "the guide says direct address; this paragraph is passive" — resolvable in minutes.
3. **It protects the brand where it matters most.** Persuasion copy — homepage, product pages, [brand films](/news/cultural-adaptation-video-content/) — lives or dies on voice. A technically perfect translation in the wrong register is a brand dilution machine running in 5 languages.
4. **New languages start on-brand from day one.** Launching market #6 no longer means rediscovering your voice; the guide's framework (confident, direct, technical-but-readable) plus per-language decisions gets added as a new section.

## Building it: the pragmatic path

**1. Start from the source voice.** Articulate your English voice first — if you can't describe it in 5 adjectives with examples, neither can your translators. Pull 3–5 exemplary existing texts (the homepage, a great product page) and mark what makes them *yours*: sentence length, humor level, how you address the reader, how you handle claims.

**2. Decide per language, with natives.** The German version of "punchy and direct" isn't literal punchiness — it's what confident-and-direct *sounds like* in business German (which tolerates more directness than Japanese, less than American English). This calibration needs a native senior translator or your in-country team — the same people who run [in-context review](/news/in-context-translation-review/) — and their decisions get written down as the language sections.

**3. Write do/don't pairs, not essays.** "Say: 'Install the valve in under ten minutes.' Don't say: 'The installation of the valve can be accomplished within a timeframe of approximately ten minutes.'" Ten concrete pairs teach more than three pages of adjectives.

**4. Cover the mechanics explicitly.** Numbers, dates, units, and capitalization cause the most visible inconsistencies and are the easiest to fix by rule. Include how to handle [text expansion](/news/text-expansion-translation-layout/) constraints in UI and marketing copy ("CTA buttons: max 20 characters in DE").

**5. Govern it with the termbase.** One owner, version-controlled, distributed with every project brief, updated from review feedback. The style guide and termbase are siblings — voice and vocabulary — and should live, version, and ship together.

## The multilingual notes

- **Formality is the highest-stakes dial.** Wrong Sie/du in German, wrong keigo level in Japanese, wrong register in [Korean](/news/korean-market-digital-expectations/) — each is instantly visible to native readers and invisible to you. These deserve the guide's most explicit sections, with examples.
- **Humor and idioms get a policy, not a ban.** Usually: recreate the *effect*, not the joke; when in doubt, go straight.
- **The guide applies beyond translation.** Original content written directly in-market (your German distributor's landing pages) should follow it too — one voice includes locally-authored content.
