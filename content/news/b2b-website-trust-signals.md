Every B2B supplier website answers one question before all others: *can I risk putting this company in front of my boss?* The buyer has never seen your factory. Their entire risk assessment comes from signals on your site — and the absence of a signal is itself a signal. Here are the ten that carry the decision, ranked by how procurement actually weights them, plus the anti-signals that quietly disqualify you.

## Tier 1: Verifiable facts (the shortlist makers)

**1. Certifications with specifics.** "ISO 9001:2015, certificate #44123, issued by TÜV SÜD, valid through 2027" is a trust signal. A generic ISO logo is decoration. Buyers in regulated industries (medical, automotive, aerospace) verify certificates; give them the number to verify.

**2. Equipment list with brands and quantities.** "14 CNC machining centers (8× DMG MORI, 6× Haas), 2× Zeiss CMM" tells a buyer in one line what you can make, at what scale, with what process discipline. This single element separates manufacturers from trading companies — which is exactly why buyers look for it.

**3. Factory evidence.** Real photos: the floor, the machines with your logo visible somewhere in frame, the inspection lab, the team. Video if you have it. One genuine walkthrough video outweighs fifty stock photos — buyers have seen the same "diverse engineers pointing at a screen" image on a hundred sites.

**4. Export track record.** "Exporting since 2011; 60% of output to Germany and the US" answers the buyer's real question: *has someone like me already taken this risk?* Name the markets, name the industries, anonymize the customers if NDAs require ("a Tier-1 automotive supplier in Michigan").

## Tier 2: Operational credibility (the reassurance layer)

**5. Named humans with contact details.** A sales engineer with a photo, name, direct email, and WhatsApp converts better than any form. Senior buyers especially route around anonymous inboxes — as we covered in the [homepage audit](/news/b2b-homepage-first-impression/), contact friction filters out exactly the people you want.

**6. Response-time commitment.** "We reply within 24 hours" — and then doing it. It costs one line of copy and sets you apart from the majority of suppliers who answer in three days or never.

**7. Case studies with numbers.** Problem → what you delivered → measurable outcome. Two solid case studies outperform an entire news section of company outings. This is the highest-leverage content a manufacturer can publish, full stop — see our [copywriting guide](/news/english-copywriting-manufacturer-websites/) for the structure.

**8. Process transparency.** A simple diagram of how you handle an RFQ — quote in 48h, DFM feedback, samples in 2 weeks, production — reduces the buyer's perceived risk of engagement itself. Uncertainty about *what happens next* kills inquiries even when capability is proven.

## Tier 3: Consistency signals (the subconscious audit)

**9. Professional, current, consistent site.** Correct English, current copyright year, recent content, working links, consistent spelling. Buyers read sloppiness as a proxy for quality systems: a company that can't maintain a website, the reasoning goes, may not maintain process control either. Unfair — and universal. Even the [visual layer](/news/image-icon-color-localization/) feeds this judgment.

**10. Physical address and company registration.** A verifiable factory address (map-embedded), business license number where customary, founding year. Anything that makes you findable in the real world lowers the fear of disappearing mid-order.

## The anti-signals

These actively destroy trust, and they're common:

- Stock photos presented as your factory or team (buyers reverse-image-search)
- "Leading manufacturer" claims with zero evidence attached
- A news section whose last update is 2023
- Gmail/QQ addresses instead of domain email
- No prices, no lead times, no MOQ guidance *anywhere* — total information asymmetry reads as "we'll quote whatever we think you'll pay"
- Certificates displayed as low-resolution, cropped images that can't be read (looks like hiding expiry dates)

## Implementation order

If you're fixing an existing site: Tier 1 first — certifications, equipment list, real photos, export record can be live in two weeks. Tier 2 next — the case studies take the longest because they require customer permission. Tier 3 is continuous maintenance. And run the [ten-second test](/news/b2b-homepage-first-impression/) after each change: a trust signal that isn't visible doesn't exist.
