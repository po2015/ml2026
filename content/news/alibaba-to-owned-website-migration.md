A manufacturer's export revenue runs 80% through their Alibaba store — five years of operation, thousands of inquiries, a solid response-rate discipline. And yet the ceiling is visible: buyers filter by price, ten competitors sit one click away inside every listing, the storefront looks like every other Gold Supplier's, and the customer list — the actual relationships — lives inside the platform's messaging system, not the company's CRM. When the annual renewal quote arrives with another fee increase, the owner asks the question every successful marketplace seller eventually asks: what would we *own* if we built our own site? The answer, done right: a brand buyers can find and trust independently, Google traffic that compounds instead of renting, and customer relationships on your own infrastructure. The answer, done wrong: an expensive brochure nobody visits. The difference is the migration plan.

## What the marketplace actually gives you (be honest first)

Alibaba and its peers solve real problems: **traffic** (buyers are already there, searching), **trust scaffolding** (trade assurance, verified-supplier badges that substitute for reputation you haven't built), and **simplicity** (no SEO, no hosting, no content strategy — just listings and response discipline). Any migration plan that ignores this ends with a beautiful owned site and an empty inbox.

What the platform never gives you: **the buyer relationship** (communication, data, and re-marketing live in their walled garden), **price insulation** (side-by-side comparison is the platform's core design), **brand equity** (buyers remember "Alibaba supplier," not your name), and **leverage** (fees and rules change unilaterally; your visibility is rented annually).

## The strategic frame: graduation, not abandonment

The healthy end-state for most exporters is **both channels, different jobs**:

- **Marketplace** = acquisition for price-shopping buyers and a trust ramp for new market entrants. Keep it running, keep it disciplined.
- **Owned website** = brand home, Google/search presence, content hub, the destination you send *every* buyer to (from fair badges, emails, and yes, marketplace conversations where rules allow), and the system of record for [inquiry handling](/news/export-website-contact-channels/) and re-marketing.

Over 2–3 years the mix typically shifts from 80/20 marketplace to 40/60 owned — not because the marketplace shrinks, but because the owned channel grows compounding assets (rankings, email list, brand searches) while the marketplace sells you the same visibility each year.

## The migration sequence

**Phase 1 — Build the owned asset properly (months 1–3).** This is where most migrations die, by building a thin site: five pages, stock photos, "leading manufacturer" copy. A site that converts marketplace-grade buyers needs the full stack: a [structured product catalog](/news/product-catalog-website-inquiry-structure/) with real specs, [trust signals](/news/b2b-website-trust-signals/) that substitute for platform badges (certifications, factory evidence, customer proof), working [RFQ flows](/news/rfq-form-design-export-websites/), and the languages of your actual markets ([the language decision](/news/how-many-languages-export-website/)). It must also be fast where your buyers are — [global speed](/news/export-website-speed-cdn-performance/) is table stakes, and marketplace-conditioned buyers bounce in seconds.

**Phase 2 — Seed traffic and trust (months 2–9).** An owned site starts with zero authority; fill the gap deliberately:
- **Content**: publish the [long-tail, spec-driven content](/news/industrial-long-tail-keywords/) marketplace listings can't hold — selection guides, application notes, comparison pages. This is what Google ranks and what engineers bookmark.
- **Redirect your own touchpoints**: email signatures, business cards, fair materials, packaging, and social profiles all point to the owned domain from day one.
- **Build the [link foundation](/news/b2b-backlink-building-exporters/)**: directories, associations, fair exhibitor pages — the systematic tier any new domain needs.
- **Optional accelerant**: small-budget search ads on your core product terms buy visibility while SEO matures — rent traffic from Google while you build owning it.

**Phase 3 — Migrate relationships, not just traffic (months 6–24).** Past marketplace customers are your warmest owned-channel audience: move them to direct email relationships (quoting from your domain, newsletters, direct re-order paths), so repeat business flows through infrastructure you control. Track the metrics that matter per channel: inquiry volume *and quality*, close rate, and customer-acquisition cost — the owned site's numbers look worse for 6–12 months and better thereafter; judge it on trajectory, not week one.

## The pitfalls that sink migrations

1. **Killing the marketplace too early.** The owned site takes 6–12 months to carry meaningful inquiry flow; cutting the platform at month 3 is how "migration" becomes "revenue gap."
2. **Building the brochure site.** A five-page brand site cannot replace a lead-generating machine; the catalog-and-content depth *is* the machine.
3. **Expecting marketplace rules to help.** Platforms prohibit moving conversations off-platform aggressively; plan transitions through your own touchpoints (samples, documentation, after-sales), not through messages that get you penalized.
4. **Treating the website as a project, not a channel.** The marketplace forced daily discipline (response time, listing updates); the owned site needs its own operating rhythm — [maintenance](/news/website-maintenance-plan-exporters/), content, analytics — or it quietly becomes the expensive brochure.
