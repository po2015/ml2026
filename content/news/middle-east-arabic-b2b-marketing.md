The Gulf states are spending heavily on industrial diversification — Saudi Vision 2030, the UAE's manufacturing push, Qatar's infrastructure pipeline — and much of that spending flows through B2B procurement that starts online. For exporters, this is one of the few major markets where competition for buyer attention is still thin. It is also a market where a translated website is the entry ticket, not the strategy.

Selling into the Middle East online requires adapting three layers: language, content, and cultural expectations. Here is what each layer actually demands.

## Language: Arabic first, English second — and which Arabic

English is widely spoken in Gulf business circles, and many exporters conclude that an English site suffices. The data disagrees: Arabic-language content consistently outperforms English in search visibility and engagement across the region, and government-linked procurement — a huge share of Gulf B2B spending — increasingly expects Arabic documentation. The practical split:

- **Website core (homepage, products, company, contact)**: Arabic and English, full parity.
- **Technical documentation**: English is acceptable to most engineering buyers; Arabic versions are a differentiator, not a requirement.
- **The Arabic itself**: Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) for all written content. Dialects (Gulf, Egyptian) belong in spoken video and informal social content, never on a corporate website. This is one case where the "formal" choice is also the safe one across 20+ countries.

Do not forget the technical layer: Arabic is right-to-left, so the site layout must genuinely mirror — navigation, forms, and icons included. A site that merely displays Arabic text inside a left-to-right layout signals immediately that the market is an afterthought.

## Content: what Gulf buyers look for

Gulf B2B buying is relationship-driven, and your content's job is to earn the meeting, not close the sale. That changes what works:

1. **Company credibility over product specs.** Buyers want to know who you are: history, scale, existing clients, and especially *regional presence*. A Dubai office, a Saudi distributor, or completed regional projects outweigh any product feature list. If you have none yet, say who your global reference clients are.
2. **Certifications and compliance**, stated precisely. Gulf industrial procurement leans heavily on ISO, API, SASO (Saudi), and ESMA (UAE) conformity. Vague "international standards" phrasing reads as evasive here, just as it does in [Germany](/news/german-market-b2b-buyer-expectations/).
3. **Long-term partnership signals.** Gulf business culture values commitment: warranty terms, after-sales support, spare parts availability, and training offered. Content that addresses "what happens after we buy" converts better than content about price.
4. **Project case studies over generic testimonials.** A named project — "supplied valve assemblies for a desalination plant in Jubail" — is worth a page of adjectives.

## Culture: the details that build or break trust

- **Imagery**: modest dress, no alcohol-related contexts, mixed-gender imagery used thoughtfully. Imagery that is unremarkable in Europe can cost you a shortlist spot.
- **Calendar awareness**: the work week runs Sunday–Thursday in most Gulf states; Ramadan shifts business rhythms dramatically (shorter hours, evening activity). Scheduling campaigns, launches, and follow-ups around this shows fluency; ignoring it shows the opposite.
- **Communication style**: business writing in Arabic is more formal and more relational than Anglo directness. Arabic marketing copy that reads as translated English — clipped, aggressive, feature-stacked — feels off. Invest in transcreation for headlines and value propositions, even where body copy can be translated more directly.
- **Names and honorifics**: getting titles right (Sheikh, Eng., Dr.) matters in direct communication and in any content quoting or addressing people.

## Channels: where Gulf B2B buyers actually search

Google dominates search across the Gulf, so international SEO fundamentals apply — with Arabic keyword research done natively, not translated from your English list. Two regional specifics:

- **LinkedIn is the strongest B2B social channel** in the Gulf, with reach into exactly the procurement and engineering roles you want — unusually effective compared to most markets.
- **WhatsApp is a business channel**, not just consumer chat. Gulf buyers commonly expect to continue inquiries there; offering it as a contact option (with a real responder) measurably lifts inquiry rates.
- Trade shows still matter enormously (GITEX, ADIPEC, Big 5) — your Arabic content should exist partly to support pre-show outreach and post-show follow-up.

## A realistic entry sequence

1. **Localize the website core into MSA Arabic** with a genuinely mirrored RTL layout — this alone puts you ahead of most exporters.
2. **Publish two or three substantial case studies** with regional relevance.
3. **Do native Arabic keyword research** for your product category and build content around how Gulf engineers actually search.
4. **Add WhatsApp as a contact channel** and staff it.
5. **Plan one regional trade show** and create Arabic landing pages for it.

The Gulf rewards companies that show up prepared and stays loyal to them for years. The upfront investment is modest — a well-localized Arabic core site is a fraction of what a single regional contract returns.
