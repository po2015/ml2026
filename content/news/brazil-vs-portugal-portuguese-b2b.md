An exporter gets a Portuguese translation done by a Lisbon-based agency, then wonders why Brazilian distributors — 85% of the addressable market — say the site "feels foreign." The reverse happens too: a Portugal-targeted campaign built on Brazilian Portuguese reads to Portuguese buyers as sloppy, sometimes comically so. This is the same pattern as [French across its markets](/news/french-localization-variants/) and [Spanish across Latin America and Spain](/news/latin-america-spanish-variants/) — one language, multiple market realities — but Portuguese has a twist: the asymmetry is extreme, and for most exporters the decision is obvious once the numbers are on the table.

## The market asymmetry

| | Brazil | Portugal |
|---|---|---|
| Population | ~215 million | ~10 million |
| Economy | Top-10 global economy; huge industrial base (agribusiness, automotive, mining, oil & gas, machinery) | Mid-size EU economy; gateway role for some EU niches |
| B2B relevance for exporters | Massive — domestic industry imports machinery, components, instruments at scale | Real but modest; procurement often comfortable in English |
| English tolerance in B2B | Low — Portuguese expected | Higher, especially in tech and EU-facing sectors |
| Digital context | Mobile-heavy, WhatsApp is the business backbone | European stack: email, desktop, SEPA |

For a machinery, components, or industrial exporter, **Brazil is the Portuguese-speaking market** — the numbers aren't close. Portugal matters for specific EU strategies, and Portuguese also reaches Angola and Mozambique (growth markets with their own infrastructure quirks), but the build order for most exporters is: PT-BR first, PT-PT if and when Portugal/EU demand justifies it.

## How the variants actually differ

Written PT-BR and PT-PT are mutually intelligible — but "intelligible" isn't "comfortable," and B2B credibility lives in the comfortable zone:

- **Pronouns and address.** Brazil runs on *você* (even in formal contexts); Portugal uses *o senhor/a senhora* or the third person in formal address, and *tu* elsewhere. A PT-PT site using *tu* with business buyers reads as presumptuous; PT-BR *você* is universally safe in Brazil.
- **Vocabulary.** *Trem* vs. *comboio* (train), *ônibus* vs. *autocarro* (bus), *celular* vs. *telemóvel* (mobile phone), *tela* vs. *ecrã* (screen), *equipe* vs. *equipa* (team). In industrial B2B the divergence is smaller — technical terms are often shared or anglicized identically — but UI copy, contact forms, and marketing prose expose the variant constantly.
- **Grammar habits.** Brazil prefers gerunds (*estamos trabalhando*), Portugal the infinitive (*estamos a trabalhar*); object pronoun placement differs (*me envie* vs. *envie-me*). These are the markers that make a text read as "from here" or "from there" within two sentences.
- **Forms and checkout details.** Brazil: CNPJ/CPF fields, CEP postal codes, BRL, and *cadastro* (registration). Portugal: NIF, EU VAT rules, EUR. Getting these wrong doesn't just look foreign — it can break procurement workflows, since Brazilian buyers often need a CNPJ field to complete an RFQ internally.

## The one-build or two-build decision

Unlike French (where [one build plus a Quebec review layer](/news/french-localization-variants/) covers most needs), Portuguese usually resolves to a cleaner answer:

**If Brazil is the target: build PT-BR, period.** Don't compromise-build "neutral Portuguese" — it exists in academia, not in market. A PT-BR site serves Brazil natively and remains *readable* in Portugal, Angola, and Mozambique.

**If Portugal is also strategically real** — significant distributor network, EU-manufacturing customers — then a PT-PT review layer applies: a Portuguese-native reviewer adapting pronouns, vocabulary, and form fields. Budget it at 20–30% of the original translation, as with other variant layers. Serve it as `pt-PT` vs `pt-BR` in your [hreflang setup](/news/hreflang-mistakes-multilingual-b2b/) — Google honors the distinction, and mixing them up makes both versions rank worse.

## Brazil-specific notes beyond language

Language is the entry ticket; three market traits shape the rest:

- **WhatsApp is infrastructure.** Brazilian B2B runs on WhatsApp even more than other [messenger-first markets](/news/export-website-contact-channels/) — a WhatsApp Business contact option is close to mandatory, and "call me" CTAs underperform it.
- **Import culture is relationship-heavy.** Brazilian buyers expect conversation before commitment; RFQ forms should feel like the start of a relationship ([short, human](/news/rfq-form-design-export-websites/)), not a procurement portal.
- **"Custo Brasil" shapes buying logic.** Import taxes, bureaucracy, and logistics complexity mean buyers scrutinize landed cost and supplier reliability hard — content that addresses documentation, Incoterms experience, and Mercosur shipping reality builds the [trust](/news/b2b-website-trust-signals/) that generic marketing copy can't.

Portuguese is one language with one obvious primary market for exporters.
